# MALLAM TOWN — Editorial Notes & Reader's Report

**Author:** Lecturer Sam · **Manuscript:** *Mallam Town: A Novel* (50pp, ~15,900 words of prose + ~2,000 words of glossary & study notes)
**Read as:** a school text with a declared audience of "basic school students"

---

## A. Verdict first

This is a genuinely gripping piece of work. The premise — a wife who didn't disappear, she left, and then let her husband take the blame — is not a cheap twist. It's a moral idea, and it's the kind of idea a classroom can argue about for an hour. The craft is much stronger than most self-published school fiction: the motifs (gates, hands, perfume, photographs, "Okay", 11:42 p.m.) are planted early and paid off late, and the ending redefining "Peacemaker" is excellent.

But right now the manuscript is **a strong draft with a real editing problem**, and the editing problem is not stylistic — it's structural. There are two full scenes duplicated wholesale, one hard factual contradiction inside a clue the reader is invited to solve, and a timeline that cannot be reconciled as printed. Those are the things that will get noticed in a classroom, because students *do* catch them.

And there is a bigger question underneath everything, which I've put first because it determines most of the advice below.

---

## B. The audience question (please settle this before anything else)

You said the target is **basic school students**. In Ghana, "basic school" = KG + Primary 1–6 + JHS 1–3 (roughly ages 4–15, ending in the BECE).

Your own paratext says otherwise, twice:

- Glossary: *"For SHS Literature students"*
- Study Notes: *"for WAEC / SHS Literature"*

SHS is not basic school. So one of the two is wrong, and you should decide now, because it changes the book.

**What the text itself measures at:** I ran readability on the prose (excluding the notes).

| Measure | Score | Roughly means |
|---|---|---|
| Flesch Reading Ease | **77.9** | "Fairly easy" — plain-English territory |
| Flesch–Kincaid Grade | **5.1** | ~US grade 5 |
| Gunning Fog | **6.9** | ~grade 7 |
| SMOG | **8.4** | ~grade 8–9 |
| Avg. sentence length | **10.9 words** | very short |
| Polysyllabic words | **7.9%** | low |

**So: the language is comfortably readable by JHS 2–3 and up.** Vocabulary and syntax are not your obstacle — a P6 pupil could decode most of this. Your obstacle is **thematic maturity**, not difficulty.

My recommendation: **aim the floor at JHS 3 / SHS 1 (age 14+)**, state that plainly on the copyright page ("Recommended for readers 14+"), and change the paratext to say JHS 3 / SHS 1 or simply "SHS". If you truly want Primary 4–6, that is a different book — it needs a rewrite, not a trim.

## C. Must-fix errors (ranked)

These are not opinions. Each is a defect a sharp student or examiner will find.

**1. Chapter 14 is printed twice, in full.** Pages 33–35 and again 35–37 (~800 duplicated words). The two copies differ only in quote style — curly vs. straight — which suggests two documents were merged. Delete one copy and normalise quotation marks throughout.

**2. The taxi-rank scene in Chapter 1 exists in two competing drafts, back to back.** Pages 6–7: Version A (the "two shots / three shots" argument) and Version B (the "blue shirt" passenger dispute), each ending on its own "Peacemaker" line. Keep the **shots** version — it pays off the prologue the reader has just read — and give Kwame one line settling the actual dispute, so the scene earns his nickname rather than only showing his knowledge. Cut the other.

**3. The phone clue contradicts itself.** p. 9: *"His phone was cold under his palm."* p. 23: *"the screen had been warm. Not cold. Warm, as if hands had just set it down."* This is the clue the reader is invited to solve, so the contradiction is fatal. Decide — **warm** is the better clue — and rewrite p. 9 to match.

**4. "Commander Wan Driver Nanfuri" — a name collision.** *Wan Driver* and *Tree Driver* are your two rank drivers, but the name has leaked into the Commander's (6 occurrences). Also p. 39: *"Put **Tree Driver Asante's** description out to every mechanic in Accra"* — you mean Kwame. And p. 12: Abena says she found him *"near **Wan Driver Lane**"* — you mean Dangerous Lane. **Root cause:** "Wan Driver" and "Tree Driver" read as un-replaced placeholders (One / Three). Give both men real Ghanaian names and do a clean find-and-replace.

**5. Stray Chinese characters.** p. 42, in Inspector Mensah's dialogue: *"marital account,**共同** phone"*. Delete.

**6. The prologue is spliced together out of order.** On p. 5, the Caretaker's paragraph is inserted in the middle of Nanfuri's scene, which then resumes as if uninterrupted — and Nanfuri *"finally lowered his weapon"* two paragraphs after he was already standing at the gate. Then: *"And he stepped through. He did not move. He did not blink."* (stepped through, then did not move). Then: *"He turned and walked deeper into the graves, **leaving no footprints in the dust**."* That last line is a ghost image, and it contradicts the grounded realism of everything after it. Untangle the order, and cut the footprints.

**7. The timeline cannot be reconciled.** Five separate contradictions:

| Source | Says | Conflict |
|---|---|---|
| p. 2 | Chase: *"4:00 a.m. Monday morning"* | Placed immediately before Ch. 1 |
| p. 5 | *"The Following Morning"* | Forces the disappearance to be Monday-night — but Ch. 5 says **Saturday** |
| p. 17 | First broadcast: *"Thursday night, ten days after"* | Saturday + 10 days = Tuesday |
| p. 32/34 | Inspector: *"The cemetery chase: three weeks before Efua died"* | But the chase is 1–2 days before she died as currently ordered |
| p. 34 | Chase = *"twenty-eight minutes"* | p. 4 says *"nearly thirty minutes"* |

**The fix is one move:** make the prologue a true prologue with its own dateline — *"Three weeks earlier. Monday, 4:00 a.m."* — and label it **PROLOGUE**. Your study notes already call it one. Everything then falls into place (see §D).

**8. Two things are promised and never delivered.**
- **The gate that did not scream** is your Chapter 2 title and a headline symbol, but it is never explained and the ending says the gate *"still hadn't been oiled."* Best fix: let the **Old Woman on the Veranda** be the one who reveals Mansa oiled it that evening. It pays off the title, it uses the Greek-chorus character you built, and it's the kind of detail students love finding.
- **The Old Woman** saw Kwame leave *and* would have seen Mansa leave. She is the one witness who could crack the case, and she is never asked.

**9. Nanfuri is 46 but has "thirty years in the service."** Joined at 16. Make it twenty-two years, or make him 52.

**10. "Commander" is not a Ghana Police Service rank.** The GPS runs Constable → … → Inspector → Chief Inspector → ASP → DSP → Superintendent → … → Commissioner. "Commander" is UK/Metropolitan. Ghanaian readers and WAEC examiners will notice. Use **"Superintendent Nanfuri"** (or DSP/ASP) and keep "commander" only as a role: *"the Mallam Division Commander."*

**11. Smaller items.** "buzzling" (p. 2 — not a word; "bustling"?) · semi-colons used as commas in *"two drivers; Wan Driver and Tree Driver;"* (p. 6) · **Chapter 1 has no heading** — the Part One page carries "The Peacemaker" but no "Chapter 1" · **"INSPECTOR —"** appears with an unfinished character placeholder (p. 32) and isn't named "Mensah" until Ch. 15 — name him at first appearance · spelling is mixed: *realis/realiz, recognis/recogniz, tyres vs. "tire iron", neighbour* vs *color*-family absences — pick British/Ghanaian and enforce.

*One thing that is probably **not** an error:* "V oomm", "V oices", "V oyeur" appear with a stray space in my text extraction. That's a font-kerning artifact of PDF extraction, almost certainly invisible in your Word file — but glance at it once to be sure.

## E. Craft

### What's working

- **The twist is an idea, not a trick.** Mansa was never missing. That's a statement about how communities assign blame, and it's arguable.
- **"Bored, not afraid."** Indifference as the true villainy — a wife discussing her husband's destruction *"the way a person discusses the weather"* — is far more disturbing, and far more teachable, than a wicked wife. Genuinely sophisticated.
- **Abena is an agent, not a reward.** *"You're not asking. I'm offering. There's a difference."* is the best line in the book, and it's a lesson about consent and agency that you should protect at all costs. Note it survives every fix I've proposed above — I'm asking you to change the *tactic*, not her *agency*.
- **The symbolic architecture is deliberate and it holds.** Watching/being watched: photograph → peephole → bug → wanted photo → press cameras. Gates: silent → hidden panel → oiled → the final gate that finally screams, *"This time, everybody heard."* That last beat is a perfect close.
- **"Not anymore."** Redefining "Peacemaker" from *the man who keeps quiet* to *the man who tells the truth* is a moral that lands without preaching. For a school text, this is the whole point of the book.
- **The Ghanaian texture is the book's identity** — MoMo, trotro, kenkey, chop bar, FanIce, the assemblyman's unfulfilled streetlight promise, the MTN shop, "Ei", "chaley", "sa". Specific, unforced, and not a single line of it feels written for tourists.
- **"Not the face. Leave his hands."** Chilling, and it says more about organised crime than a page of exposition would.
- **The institutional critique has teeth:** two boys vanish and nobody looks; a commander's daughter dies and the whole system moves. Kwame demanding *all* the names at the press conference is the book's conscience.

### What needs trimming

- **A handful of constructions are doing too much work.** In 15,900 words: *"as if"* ×23, *"deliberate(ly)/practised"* ×15, *"Ordinary"* ×13, *"the way a man/woman…"* ×9, *"the kind of [person] who…"* ×8. In an adult novel that's voice. In a book read aloud in class it becomes a mannerism. Cut them by a third.
- **The narration explains its own effects too often.** *"It was the single most devastating acting choice in the entire story"* (p. 28) breaks the fourth wall with film language — in a novel, delete it and let the scene do the work. Same for *"the kind of argument that had clearly started somewhere else"*, *"This was worse, and everyone in the compound knew it"*. Trust the scene; your reader is smarter than the commentary.
- **Screenplay residue.** *"FADE OUT. / THE END"* (p. 45). Also the ALL-CAPS character introductions (KWAME ASANTE, MANSA ASANTE, KOFI DAPAAH, YAW SARPONG, INSPECTOR —) and the *Act One / Act Two / Act Three* labels in your notes versus *Part One / Part Two / Part Three* in the text. Commit to one set of conventions.
- **Sentence rhythm.** 1,504 sentences; 36% are under six words; average 10.9. Accessible, yes — but the staccato is unrelieved. Deliberately vary: give the big emotional moments longer sentences.
- **Two characters are thinner than they should be.**
  - **Mansa** is your most interesting character and the least seen. She has no interior life until Ch. 17, which is very late. **One 300-word scene in her POV** — placed early, even before we know what she's done — would make the confrontation land three times harder and stop her being a plot device.
  - **Sarpong** is pure "comic-careless" villain. One small scene showing him *calculating* would raise the stakes everywhere.
- **Nanfuri's turnaround is the emotional climax and it happens off-stage in summary.** A grieving father who put an innocent man on national television, learning he was wrong — that is the most powerful scene in your book, and right now it's two pages of an Inspector explaining. Consider dramatising it properly.
- **One coincidence needs a line of cover.** Abena plants the bug in Sarpong's back room and Mansa happens to turn up within three nights. Give us one sentence — a tenant mentions "the woman who comes on Fridays", or Abena overhears him on the phone — and it stops being luck.
- **Naming is inconsistent.** The gang is *"Three Strangers"*, *"the three from Dangerous Lane"*, *"the carjack people"*, and *"the syndicate"* by turns. Pick one.

### A positioning note

At ~16,000 words this is a **novella**, not a novel, and SHS/WAEC set texts are usually 40,000+. Two honest options: **(a)** embrace it — there is a real market in Ghana for 60–80 page supplementary readers for JHS, and at this length and reading level it fits that slot beautifully; or **(b)** expand toward 30–35,000 words, which also gives you room for the Mansa POV scene, the Nanfuri scene, and the missing boys' resolution. Either is legitimate. Just choose, and label it accordingly ("A Novella" / "A Supplementary Reader").

## F. Glossary & Study Notes

Good instincts — most school fiction has neither, and WAEC-style notes sell books to teachers. Fixes:

1. **Wrong audience label.** "For SHS Literature students" / "for WAEC / SHS Literature" contradicts "basic school". Change to match whatever you decide in §B.
2. **Numbering jumps 5 → 8** (Setting → Context). Sections 6 and 7 are missing.
3. **Two orphan glossary entries.** *"something small"* (bribe) and *"WAEC"* never appear in the novel. Either drop them, or — better — plant *"something small"* in the text ("the police at the junction collect something small every Tuesday"), which strengthens your institutional-corruption theme.
4. **"Prologue" and "Act One/Two/Three"** in the notes vs. "Part One/Two/Three" in the novel. Standardise on the novel's terms.
5. **The notes spoil the entire plot.** They're at the back, which is right, but add a one-line spoiler warning at the top ("Read the novel first").
6. **Missing: discussion questions.** This is what teachers actually buy. Add 8–12 questions per Part, mixing comprehension with argument (*"Is Mansa's punishment fair?"* / *"Why does Kwame refuse to be called Peacemaker anymore?"*).
7. **Consider adding:** a short "Before you read" page, an "About the author" note, and a one-line content note ("Recommended for readers 14+").
