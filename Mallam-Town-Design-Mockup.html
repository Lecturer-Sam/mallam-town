02 — Spine and back

MALLAM TOWN · LECTURER SAM
Back cover

Kwame Asante is the man everyone in Mallam Town brings their quarrels to. Twenty years at the taxi rank, and they call him the Peacemaker.

Then one Monday night his wife Mansa disappears, his taxi is taken, and he wakes in a gutter with a shoulder out of its socket and no way to prove any of it.

A week later his face is on the radio. The police say he robbed and killed a young woman. The whole town believes them — because the whole town has always found it easier to believe the simple thing.

Only two people do not. One is an old woman with bad knees who treats people with plants and has been listening to this town for forty years. The other is a young police inspector who has read every page of a file nobody asked her to read.

Mallam Town is a story about a town that has to learn to look at what is in front of it — and a man who has to learn that keeping the peace and keeping quiet are not the same thing.

Chapter One
The Peacemaker

Mallam Town woke up at four and did not wait for anybody. By seven the taxi rank was already full of dust and shouting and horns. Old yellow taxis nosed in one after another, their paint faded by the sun. Drivers leaned against bonnets arguing about fuel prices.

Kwame Asante sat on an overturned plastic crate beside his own taxi, polishing his side mirror with a rag. The rag was far cleaner than the car deserved.

The illustration sits at the head of the chapter, 106 × 62 mm, the full width of the type area, with the chapter number, title and rule beneath it. The text starts below that.

Why not a full-page plate facing the opening? It is more beautiful, and if the budget allows it is the upgrade I would take. But it adds fifteen pages, and at this age the picture does more work next to the words it opens than across the gutter from them. The half-page panel keeps the book at 80 pp and keeps the cue exactly where a struggling reader needs it.

Three-line drop cap in ochre, chapter openings only — the one ornament in the book.

06 — A text pageVerso and recto

Mallam Town

A kenkey seller went past calling out her prices. At the corner a preacher's public address system coughed into life, far too early, and a hundred people pretended not to hear it.

When the preacher got going, Kwame listened for a while, and then opened the bible on his knee to check the verse. He did this most mornings. Not to argue — to see whether the man was telling the truth. If he found that he was not, Kwame simply stopped listening.

He was forty-four, tall and lanky, with quiet hands and a face that did not hurry to show what he was thinking. Twenty years at this rank had taught him that most arguments are not about what they say they are about, and that nearly all of them go quiet on their own if nobody feeds them.

Peace, in Kwame's life, meant keeping quiet long enough for other people's noise to die down.

He did not yet know how much that belief was going to cost him.

07 — Back matterGlossary

Glossary
Nene (NEH-neh)
A Ga-Dangme word. It is a title of respect, used for a chief, a queen-mother, or for any elder. In this book it is not really Nene's name: “Nene was what you called an old woman you were about to ask a favour of.”
okada
A motorcycle that carries passengers for money, the way a taxi does. Motorcycle taxis are called okada across West Africa. They are fast, they are cheap, and they are dangerous.
compound house
A house built around an open yard, with the rooms all opening onto that yard. Several families usually live in one compound and share the yard, the kitchen and the bathhouse. The yard is the place where everybody sees everybody.
kenkey
A Ghanaian food made from fermented maize dough, wrapped in corn husks or plantain leaves and boiled. Usually eaten with fried fish and pepper sauce.
