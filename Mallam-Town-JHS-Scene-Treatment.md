# PART ONE — THE DISAPPEARANCE

## Chapter 1 — The Peacemaker
**800 words · POV: omniscient, settling on Kwame · Monday, dawn to 11:42 p.m.**

**Scene 1 — Dawn at the rank (200 w)**
Mallam Town waking. Taxis nosing in, faded yellow paint. Dust. Trotro horns. The kenkey seller shouting prices. A preacher's PA system starting up too early. Kwame Asante sits on an overturned crate polishing a side mirror with a rag far cleaner than the car deserves. Establish the nickname before we earn it.

**Scene 2 — The blue shirt (200 w)**
Two drivers arguing over a passenger who short-changed someone. Each insists it was the other's customer. Kwame doesn't look up. One question: *"Was the man wearing a blue shirt?"* Yes. *"Then that's the one you dropped at the junction an hour ago. Not his."* One driver walks off. The other: *"Peacemaker. Every time."* Kwame shrugs. **This is the scene that earns the nickname — keep it short and let it be funny.**

**Scene 3 — Evening (200 w)**
The compound house. Kwame home. Mansa braiding her hair on the porch — slow, deliberate, the way she does everything. A pot on the stove. **Her perfume** — sweet, strong, filling the room before you see her. She asks about the MoMo balance. Second time this month. That particular tone that isn't really a question. Kwame doesn't answer.

**Scene 4 — 11:42 p.m. (200 w)**
He walks out to cool his head — not to punish her. Past the Old Woman on the veranda; her chair creaks; she says nothing. At the compound gate he pushes slowly, muffling the sound with his hand, the way he always does. **Tonight it does not scream.**
> **End on:** Fifty metres down the road. He stops. *"My phone."*

## Chapter 2 — The Empty Room
**700 words · POV: Kwame · Monday, 11:52 p.m. – Tuesday, 12:15 a.m.**

**Scene 1 — He turns back (150 w)**
He walks back. The yard is dark. The Old Woman's chair is empty. He pushes the door open. **Wrong darkness — he left the light on.** "Mansa?"

**Scene 2 — The room (200 w)**
He finds the bedside table by memory. **The phone is warm** under his palm — not cold. Warm, as if hands had just set it down. He thumbs the screen alive. Empty bed. Empty corner. Empty chair. Then the wall switch. Light. Still empty. **Her perfume is everywhere** — recent, thick, impossible to reconcile with an empty room.

**Scene 3 — Two calls (200 w)**
He dials. **REJECTED.** He tries again. **REJECTED.** He stares at the screen. On it — the call log. **A number he does not recognise, dialled that night.** He does not look at it properly. He is too frightened to care about a number. *(Plant for Ch. 7 and Ch. 13.)*

**Scene 4 — He takes the taxi (150 w)**
Fear turning into movement, because movement is the only thing he knows how to do with fear. The engine catches on the second try.
> **End on:** *"He did not know that by going to look for his wife, he was driving into the thing that would take everything else."*

## Chapter 3 — Dangerous Lane
**750 words · POV: Kwame · Tuesday, ~00:30**

**Scene 1 — Driving (150 w)**
Dangerous Lane. Everyone knows not to stop there after dark. Kwame leans over the wheel, scanning both sides for a flash of her cloth.

**Scene 2 — Boxed in (200 w)**
Headlights flare in the mirror — too close, too fast. A second vehicle cuts in ahead and blocks the lane. He stops. Hands stay on the wheel.

**Scene 3 — Three people (250 w)**
Two men and a woman. Unhurried. One carries a tyre iron. The woman holds a pistol low against her thigh, almost casual, like a handbag. *"Step out."* He does — the same calculation he has made a hundred times with angry passengers, and it has never once favoured pride. The beating is **fast and businesslike**. Controlled, not wild.
> *"Not the face."* / *"Leave his hands."*
He won't understand why until later: taxi drivers need their hands. Need their face too, if they want to work again. **These people are not animals. They are workers, doing a job.** Dislocated shoulder. Ribs. A cut above the eye.

**Scene 4 — The gutter (150 w)**
His own engine, his own car, pulling away. He lies in the dust listening to his breathing and the insects and Mallam Town continuing somewhere beyond the lane, uncaring.
> **End on:** silence.

## Chapter 4 — Nene
**1,000 words · POV: Nene, then Kwame · Tuesday, dawn to Wednesday**

**Scene 1 — First light (200 w)**
Nene is out before the sun, because the bark she wants comes off the tree behind the cemetery and comes off easier before the day gets hot. Establish her properly: seventy-odd, knees that complain, a basket, a stick she won't admit she needs. Establish the compound: herbs drying on a line, a mortar, a calabash, the smell of bark. **Establish who she is and who she is not.** One line:
> *"Nene was not her name, not really. Nene was what you called an old woman you were about to ask a favour of."*

**Scene 2 — She finds him (200 w)**
Face-down in the gutter with one arm under him at a wrong angle. She has set shoulders before — her nephew's, the year he fell out of the mango tree; two of the market women's; one belonging to a man who never thanked her. She kneels, which takes a while and hurts, and turns him over by the good arm. *"Ei. Who did this to you?"* Then, in the light: **this is Kwame. The Peacemaker.** *"So. Even you."*

**Scene 3 — The treatment (250 w)**
Neighbours help her get him to the compound. **Show the work in full detail** — this is her characterisation. The shoulder: she has done this before, she is not squeamish, she tells him it will hurt and it does. The shoulder goes back with a sound that makes her own bones ache in sympathy. Then bark, pounded; a warm cloth; the cut cleaned. **Then her limits** — she checks his pupils, presses gently at the back of his skull, and says plainly: *"If your head were bleeding inside, no leaf would help you. That one is for the hospital. And you are lucky."*

**Scene 4 — He wakes (200 w)**
Pain before memory. The room is not his: herbs hanging, the smell of bark, a thin mattress on the floor. Nene asleep in a chair, awake the moment he moves. He tries to sit up; the room tilts. *"Lie still. You'll open the cut."*

**Scene 5 — The one exchange (150 w)**
Later. He is awake properly and, out of habit more than anything, he gives thanks — quietly, to himself. Nene hears. She shrugs, grinding bark:
> *"The one who made the tree and the one who made the leaf. You think they are different people?"*
Then, matter-of-factly, why she didn't take him to the hospital or the police:
> *"And then the police come, and they ask questions, and you are a man with bruises and no answers. I have watched that happen. I am not watching it again."*
**Never return to this. It is said once, and it is enough.**
> **End on:** Kwame: *"I have to report it anyway."* Nene: *"I know. I'll take you on Thursday."*

# PART TWO — THE LIE

## Chapter 5 — The Report
**750 words · POV: Kwame · Thursday, morning**

**Scene 1 — The walk (200 w)**
Nene walks him part of the way and waits. Kwame moves stiffly. Establish the police post: squat concrete, paint peeling, a ceiling fan turning too slowly to matter.

**Scene 2 — The ledger (200 w)**
A Desk Officer with a blue Biro that skips. *"Make and year?"* — *"Toyota. Old one. Dent on the rear passenger door. You'll know it before you ask the plate number."* *(Plant: the dent identifies the car later.)* Then: *"And the wife?"* Kwame tries to explain and can't. How do you fit it into the lines of a police form? He settles on: *"She's missing. Since Monday night."*

**Scene 3 — Chief Inspector Nanfuri (200 w)**
Through a half-open door: the senior officer, mid-forties, tired in the unremarkable way of a man with too many open files. He glances up at the mention of a name. Files it nowhere. Goes back to his paperwork. **We do not yet know what grief is sitting on him.**

**Scene 4 — Nothing happens (150 w)**
Kwame leaves with a case number on a torn slip of paper. Too thin to hold everything. Days pass. He searches the rank. Nene asks after Mansa the way old women do — between other words, while measuring bark. Nothing. **Nene was right.**
> **End on:** Kwame back at the compound, standing in the empty room. The perfume is fading.

## Chapter 6 — A Face on the Radio
**750 words · POV: Kwame and Nene · Tuesday morning, ten days in**

**Scene 1 — Ordinary morning (150 w)**
Nene's compound. Radio on low, as it always is. Kwame's shoulder is healing; he tries to pound something and can't. Nene tells him to sit down. *(Keep her ordinary — this is the antidote to the stereotype.)*

**Scene 2 — The news (200 w)**
Local station. A young woman found dead in a vehicle in Mallam Town. **Efua Nanfuri, twenty-four.** Daughter of the Chief Inspector at the Mallam Town post. Kwame's fork stops. *"A taxi."*

**Scene 3 — His own name (200 w)**
The vehicle is identified. It is his. Then his own name, and his description — the man everyone knew as calm and harmless, the Peacemaker — named an armed robber and a murderer. **A reward announced.** Listeners urged not to approach him.

**Scene 4 — The room holds it (200 w)**
Kwame: *"I can't walk into a police station now."* Nene: *"No."* No pity, no shock — that same practical quality. *"Then we find out who did it ourselves. Slowly. Properly."* Kwame asks how. Nene says she has been hearing things for a long time.
> **End on:** *"Sit down, Kwame. This will take a while, and I am not young."*

## Chapter 7 — What Nene Knows
**800 words · POV: Nene · Tuesday–Wednesday**

**Scene 1 — How she knows things (200 w)**
Nene's method, stated plainly: **people come to her, and they talk.** Not because she asks — because she is there, and because what is said in her compound goes no further. Mothers with sick children. Market women with bad backs. Men who will not go to the hospital because the hospital asks questions. **Her house is where the town's secrets arrive.**

**Scene 2 — The two young men (250 w)**
About a year ago: two apprentices from the mechanic shops near the rank — **seventeen and nineteen**, brothers to different mothers — stopped coming home. Nobody looked. Nene knows because **their mothers came to her** for medicine for sleeplessness, for worry, for the pain in their chests that wasn't their chests. She still has the bottles. *(Concrete detail: two small bottles, labelled with their names, on a shelf.)*

**Scene 3 — The construction worker (200 w)**
Six months ago, a man vanished near the uncompleted houses behind the cemetery. The police wrote it down. The family cried. Life continued.
> Nene's line — the book's moral centre: *"A chief inspector's daughter dies and the whole town wakes up. Two boys from the rank disappear and the town goes back to sleep. I have been angry about that for a long time."*

**Scene 4 — The phone (150 w)**
Nene asks to see Kwame's phone. The call log. The number he didn't recognise, dialled the night Mansa went. *"Who is this?"* He doesn't know.
> **End on:** Nene: *"Somebody used your phone that night. Somebody who did not want the call on their own."*

## Chapter 8 — Mansa
**800 words · POV: Mansa · Wednesday–Saturday**

**Scene 1 — She is alive (200 w)**
One of the uncompleted houses behind the cemetery. Not tortured, not heroic: **frightened, cold, bored, waiting.** She counts days by the light on the wall. Establish the conditions plainly and without melodrama — a room, a bucket, a door that locks from outside, someone who brings food and doesn't speak.

**Scene 2 — She hears it (200 w)**
A radio left on somewhere. Her husband's name. Armed robber. Murderer. At first she doesn't believe it — it's stupid, it's impossible, it's Kwame, who won't raise his voice. **And then she believes it.** *The moment she believes it is the worst moment in the book.* She says, to no one: *"He said nothing. He always said nothing. Maybe that was the whole thing."*

**Scene 3 — What really happened (250 w)** *(flashback, her POV)*
That night: she couldn't sleep. She walked past the uncompleted houses and saw men unloading — building materials, wire, a generator, in the dark, with torches. **She recognised one of them.** She went home. She dressed. **She put on her perfume** because she was going out to meet someone. And while Kwame was still at the rank she **oiled the compound gate** — not for what she was about to do, but because she did not want the whole compound to hear her leave, and because she was a little ashamed, and shame makes people careful about noise. *(This is why the gate did not scream for Kwame at 11:42 — see Ch. 1.)* Her own phone had no credit, so **she used Kwame's** — that is why the phone was warm. She called the wrong person. She told the wrong person what she had seen. Then she went out through the gate she had oiled, and it made no sound at all. The man who came was not the man she was expecting. First call: rejected, because they had her arm. Second call: rejected, because the phone was taken out of her hand.

**Scene 4 — On the radio (150 w)**
Later, a broadcast: a statement from the wife of the accused. Mansa's own words, taken before anyone knew anything — *"I no longer know the man I married."* Not a curse. Just grief that has picked the wrong target. Kwame hears it across town.
> **End on:** Mansa in the dark, whispering his name. **"Kwame."** — and the reader can't tell yet whether it is grief or accusation.

# PART THREE — THE TRUTH

## Chapter 9 — What Mansa Saw
**750 words · POV: Kwame and Nene · Sunday–Monday**

**Scene 1 — He hears it (150 w)**
Kwame hears Mansa's voice on the radio and it lands differently from anything so far. Not humiliation — **the knowledge that she believes he is a killer.** Nene makes him eat.

**Scene 2 — Nene's method (200 w)**
Nene doesn't theorise. **She sends for people.** She sends for the mothers of the two young men. They come, because you come when Nene sends for you. Kwame watches her work and understands for the first time what she actually is in this town.

**Scene 3 — The mothers (200 w)**
What their sons said before they vanished: a man was paying well for work at the uncompleted houses, cash, no questions, at night. One mother remembers a name her boy mentioned once, laughing, because the man was his landlord.

**Scene 4 — The landlord (200 w)**
**Yaw Sarpong.** He owns the uncompleted houses behind the cemetery. He owns the compound Kwame lives in. Kwame has paid him rent for six years. Kwame says, slowly:
> *"He came to the compound the week before it happened. Looking at the wall."*
> **End on:** Nene: *"Then we start with the wall."*

## Chapter 10 — The Plan
**800 words · POV: Kwame and Nene · Monday–Tuesday**

**Scene 1 — She proposes (200 w)**
**Nene proposes it. Not Kwame.** Keep the agency line — and note it is *better* in an old woman's mouth:
> *"You are not asking me. I am offering. There is a difference — and at my age I have earned the right to decide."*

**Scene 2 — How she moves (200 w)**
> *"Nobody stops an old woman. They do not even see me. That is the whole use of me."*
She will go to the compound, to the market, to the mothers — **asking after people, the way old women do, so that nobody hears it as questions.**

**Scene 3 — His job (200 w)**
Kwame stays hidden, and knows the streets: the alleys, the gaps in fences, the uncompleted houses he has dropped passengers at over twenty years of driving. **The parts of Mallam Town that don't appear on any map.**

**Scene 4 — The number (200 w)**
The call log. Nene knows a woman at the MTN kiosk at the rank who can tell her who a number belongs to, for nothing, because Nene once treated her child. The phone goes to market in her basket. **Two kinds of evidence: her memory and the paper. Neither is enough alone.**
> **End on:** Nene at the door with her basket. *"Lock it behind me."*

## Chapter 11 — The Carpenter
**850 words · POV: Kwame · Wednesday**

**Scene 1 — Waiting (150 w)**
Days of ordinary routine. Kwame chafes. Nene is patient. She treats a child's fever and is paid in yam and told off for charging too little. *(Keep this — ordinariness is the point.)*

**Scene 2 — Kwabena comes (200 w)**
**Kwabena Dapaah**, fifty-four, a carpenter — comes to Nene's for medicine for his hands. A man of few words whose hands speak more fluently than his mouth. Nene is mixing something at the back. Kwame is in the next room, half in shadow.

**Scene 3 — He freezes (200 w)**
Kwabena stops dead in the doorway. His knuckles go white on the handle of his basket. Five full seconds. Then, quiet, almost to himself:
> *"You're the husband."*
Then he walks out — leaving his medicine unmixed, leaving Nene staring after him.

**Scene 4 — Three nights (150 w)**
Kwabena planes the same piece of wood until it is too thin to be useful. His wife watches him from the doorway. *"You haven't slept right in three nights."* *"I'm fine."* Neither believes it.

**Scene 5 — He comes back (150 w)**
> *"I built the door."*
Not into Kwame's room. **Into the uncompleted house behind the cemetery** — a way in that you would never find unless you knew to look. Sarpong commissioned it. Paid half what he promised; the rest never came. *"I did not know what he wanted it for. Then I knew. And by then I had been paid."*
> **End on:** *"Behind the third house. There is a door in the wall. They keep things in there."*

## Chapter 12 — The Narrow Escape
**1,000 words · POV: Kwame · Thursday, dusk**

**Scene 1 — She won't go (150 w)**
Kwame insists on going tonight. Nene refuses to come with him:
> *"I am not climbing through a hole in a fence at my age. You go. I will be here when you come back, and if anyone asks, I will say I saw nothing — and they will believe me, because who tells an old woman anything?"*

**Scene 2 — The door in the wall (250 w)**
Dusk. The third uncompleted house. Kwame finds the door by touch, the way Kwabena described. Inside: **stolen goods** — bags of cement, coils of wire, a generator, and **the stripped parts of a taxi.** Building materials for houses that are never finished, made out of things that were stolen. And, in a corner: **Mansa's cloth.** A wrapper, folded, her perfume gone from it. *(Perfume motif pays off: faded everywhere except here.)* She was here. She isn't now.

**Scene 3 — He is seen (250 w)**
A car turns into the track. Slow. No headlights. **The same unhurried confidence as Dangerous Lane.** Kwame runs — through the alleys, past the food stall where the seller looks up, startled, then quickly looks away. *The Mallam Town reflex: see nothing, survive.*

**Scene 4 — Nene raises the compound (200 w)**
Cornered at the gap in the zinc fence. Men closing, unhurried. **Then: Nene's voice.** She came anyway — not running, walking, and behind her **the whole compound**: the two mothers, the market women, the old men, children, the man from the MTN kiosk. She went door to door. The men melt away, because there are suddenly **witnesses**, and witnesses are the one thing this town has never provided.

**Scene 5 — Back at the compound (150 w)**
Nene, calm: *"You thought I would sit."* Kwame sits with Mansa's wrapper in his hands.
> **End on:** a knock at the door. Not the men. **A policeman.**

## Chapter 13 — The Honest Inspector
**850 words · POV: Kwame, Nene, Mensah · Thursday night**

**Scene 1 — The knock (200 w)**
**Inspector Adzo Mensah** — one rank below Nanfuri, so the hierarchy reads correctly. She asks to come in. Kwame is ready to bolt. Nene is not; she steps aside.
> *"I am not here to arrest anyone. May I come in?"*

**Scene 2 — The file (250 w)**
Mensah has been cross-referencing dates for weeks. She has doubted the official story since the first day, and she says why:
> *"You reported that taxi stolen four days before Efua Nanfuri died. A man does not report his own murder weapon. And your injuries — I have seen the clinic record."*
*(Payoff: Nene sent him to the clinic for the head injury in Ch. 4 because she knew her limits. The record is now evidence. Her knowing what she cannot do is what saves him.)*

**Scene 3 — Two kinds of evidence (200 w)**
Nene and Mensah trade. **She has the memory** — the two young men, the construction worker, the mothers, the name. **She has the paper** — the number from Kwame's phone, traced: it belongs to a man who works for Sarpong. Neither is enough alone. Together they close.

**Scene 4 — The decision (200 w)**
Mensah: *"I cannot do this alone. My Chief Inspector will not hear it. He has already put your face on the radio, and he will not take it off again on my say-so."* So: get the proof first, then bring him to it — **in front of witnesses, in daylight, where he cannot look away.** And Kwame says it:
> *"If saying this out loud costs me something, let it cost me. I am tired of being quiet."*
> **End on:** *"Dawn,"* Nene says. *"Everything in this town happens in the dark. Let us have it in the light for once."*

## Chapter 14 — Dawn at the Uncompleted House
**1,100 words · POV: Kwame, then Nene · Friday, dawn**

**Scene 1 — The walk (150 w)**
Dawn. The whole town walking out together: Nene with her stick, the two mothers, the market women, Kwabena Dapaah with his tools, the two drivers from the rank, the Old Woman on the veranda, children who shouldn't be there. Past the cemetery. Past the gate that rusts.

**Scene 2 — Nanfuri arrives (200 w)**
Chief Inspector Nanfuri, with Mensah and two officers. Angry — this is a crowd at a crime scene. Kwame steps forward. Unarmed. Hands open, palms out.
> *"Chief Inspector. You put my face on the radio and called me a murderer. I am here to show you whose hands did it."*

**Scene 3 — The proof (250 w)**
In order, and in front of everyone:
1. **Kwabena opens the door in the wall** — with his own tools, in daylight. *"I built it."*
2. Inside: the cement, the wire, the generator — and **the stripped parts of Kwame's taxi**, with the dent on the rear passenger door.
3. And in the inner room: **Mansa.** Alive. Brought out into the daylight, blinking.

**Scene 4 — Mansa's truth (200 w)**
Said out loud, in front of the whole town: what she saw, who she called, the wrong person, the gate.
> *"I opened the gate myself. That is why it did not scream. He did not leave me. I went."*
Kwame's forgiveness is **not a speech.** It is a nod. *(Do not write the reconciliation as a scene — write it as the beginning of one.)*

**Scene 5 — Nanfuri (150 w)**
He branded an innocent man because the simplest story was the easiest one to grieve with. **He does not get absolution.** He gets the harder work.
> *"I will say it on the radio myself. Every station that carried your name will carry mine."*
Then, to Kwame: *"I am sorry."* Kwame: *"I am sorry about your daughter."* No handshake needed — or one, brief.

**Scene 6 — Not anymore (150 w)**
Kwame's line — the Peacemaker's line, earned across 14 chapters:
> *"I spent my whole life thinking peace meant keeping quiet. I don't believe that anymore."*

## Chapter 15 — The Okada
**700 words · POV: Kwame · Friday, after dawn**

**Scene 1 — Sarpong arrives (150 w)**
Sarpong comes up the track on an okada — he has been tipped off, or he has come to move what is in the third house. He sees the door standing open, the cement and the wire, the stripped panels of a taxi with a dent in the rear passenger door. He sees Mansa Asante in the daylight with somebody's coat over her shoulders. He turns and runs.

**Scene 2 — Nobody touches him (200 w)**
**The crowd simply parts and lets him through, because there is nowhere left for him to go.** *This is the moral hinge of the chapter — write it plainly.* He gets the bike moving. It fishtails on the loose gravel of the track — the track he never had surfaced, because a surfaced track is a track people remember — and goes down on its side, and Sarpong goes with it. The car coming up behind him was not going fast. It did not matter. **Write the sound, not the body.** Then dust, and silence, and every person in that crowd standing absolutely still.

**Scene 3 — Nene (200 w)**
Nene is the first to move. She goes forward leaning on her stick and kneels — which takes a while, and hurts — and puts two fingers at the side of his neck, **exactly as she did for Kwame in the gutter in Ch. 4.** Somebody says it, breathless and half-pleased:
> *"It is finished for him."*
Nene does not look up.
> *"It is not finished for him. It is beginning. He is still a man. Somebody call the hospital."*
*(This converts "disability as punishment" into the book's clearest moral beat, and it pays off her Ch. 4 refrain — "that one is for the hospital" — as mercy rather than a referral. Her knowing her limits saved Kwame in Ch. 13. Here it saves the man who ruined him.)*

**Scene 4 — The Gate (150 w)**
The ambulance comes. Inspector Adzo Mensah takes Sarpong's statement where he lies, because that is her job and she does it properly. Then the crowd walks back into Mallam Town in the morning light. **The last beat belongs to Nene, doing something ordinary** — complaining about her knees, telling a child to stop running, being paid in yam and told off for it. Because that is the whole point of her.
> **Final image:** Kwame pushes through the gate at the end of the rank. It screams — loud, rusty, impossible to ignore. **This time, everybody hears.**

1. **Villain: Yaw Sarpong**, the landlord. He owns the compound *and* the uncompleted houses; the fencing operation runs out of the houses. Keeps the crime local and graspable for 12–15s. *(Project-G's harbour syndicate is distant and abstract; this is three streets away.)*
2. **The two missing people are apprentices, aged about seventeen and nineteen**, from the mechanic shops near the rank — near your readers' own age, so it lands, but not small children, which would frighten a 12-year-old. Their families get an answer on the page in Ch. 14.
3. **Efua is a passenger.** She hailed what she thought was a taxi. She realised something was wrong. She tried to get out. Simple, horrible, believable — and it makes Nanfuri's need to blame someone entirely human. *(Replaces Project-G's "ledger files," which needed too much explaining.)*
4. **Mansa is innocent but made a mistake** — she told the wrong person. That gives Kwame something to forgive that isn't betrayal, which is what makes the reconciliation ending work.
5. **Local radio, not national television.** More authentic for Ghana, far more believable from a Chief Inspector, and it fits a world where news arrives at Nene's door.
6. **Kwabena comes to Nene for medicine for his hands**, not to fix a shelf. Integrates Nene naturally and repeats the "hands" motif.
7. **Kwame's phone is the forensic thread.** Concrete, visual, and a kind of clue a 13-year-old can follow.
