# MALLAM TOWN — Glossary

### for the JHS edition · by Lecturer Sam

---

## How to use this glossary

This book is set in Ghana, in a real township called Mallam Town, in the Greater Accra Region. Most of the words in it are ordinary English that you already know. But some words are Ghanaian, some are hard to read, and some tell you something about the people in the story.

This glossary has four parts:

- **Part A — Words from Ghana.** Food, transport, houses, names, titles and money.
- **Part B — Difficult words in this book.** Hard English words, explained simply, with the sentence from the story.
- **Part C — The names in this book.** Most of the characters are named for the day of the week they were born. You can work out which day.
- **Part D — Nene's medicine.** The words used about Nene, and the words deliberately not used.

# Part A — Words from Ghana

**Accra** *(A-kruh)* — the capital city of Ghana.

**Agbogbloshie** *(a-bog-blo-shee)* — a large, very crowded market area in central Accra. In this book, Mansa once had her purse taken there.

**apprentice** — a young person learning a trade by working under somebody who is already skilled, usually for little or no pay. Kwesi and Ekow are apprentices at a mechanic's shop.

**Chief Inspector** — a senior officer in the Ghana Police Service. Above an Inspector, below an Assistant Superintendent.

**compound / compound house** — a house built around an open yard, with the rooms all opening onto that yard. Several families usually live in one compound and share the yard, the kitchen and the bathhouse. The yard is the place where everybody sees everybody.

**Desk Officer** — the police officer at the front desk of a station, who takes reports from the public. In this book, the Desk Officer writes in a ledger with a blue Biro that skips.

**gutter** — the open drain at the side of a road. In this book the gutter is where Mallam Town puts things it does not want to look at.

**highlife** — a popular Ghanaian music style that mixes guitar bands, horns and Akan rhythms. It has been the sound of Ghana's bars and taxis for nearly a hundred years.

**Inspector** — a police officer ranking below a Chief Inspector.

**kenkey** — a Ghanaian food made from fermented maize dough, wrapped in corn husks or plantain leaves and boiled. It is usually eaten with fried fish and pepper sauce. The kenkey seller in this book walks the rank calling out her prices.

**kiosk** — a small stall or booth at the side of the road. An **MTN kiosk** sells mobile phone credit, SIM cards and Mobile Money services.

**Maame** *(MAH-meh)* — "mother" in Twi. It is also used as a respectful name for any older woman, whether or not she is really your mother. That is why the two mothers in this book are called *Maame* Adjoa and *Maame* Akua.

**Mallam Town** — a real township in the Greater Accra Region, on the road out of Accra. In this book it is the place where Kwame and Mansa live.

**MoMo** — short for **Mobile Money**: sending and receiving money on a mobile phone. Very common in Ghana. People pay for almost anything with it, and people ask each other about their MoMo balance.

**mortar and pestle** — a heavy bowl (the mortar) and a short club (the pestle), used for pounding things. Nene pounds bark into a paste in hers. The mortar in her room is worn smooth on one side from years of use.

**MTN** — one of Ghana's largest mobile phone networks.

**Nene** *(NEH-neh)* — a Ga-Dangme word. It is a title of respect, used for a chief, a queenmother, or for any elder. In this book it is not really Nene's name:

> "Nene was not her name, not really. Nene was what you called an old woman you were about to ask a favour of."

**okada** — a motorcycle that carries passengers for money, the way a taxi does. Motorcycle taxis are called okada across West Africa. They are fast, they are cheap, and they are dangerous.

**Papa** — a respectful name for an older man, whether or not he is really your father. That is why a driver at the rank is called Papa Kofi.

**police post** — a small local police station, smaller than a district station.

**rank** — the place where taxis and trotro gather to wait for passengers. Twenty years at the rank is what taught Kwame how to settle an argument.

**trotro** — a shared minibus that runs a fixed route and picks passengers up along the way. The name is said to come from the old fare of threepence.

**uncompleted house** — a building somebody started and never finished. In many Ghanaian towns you can see them standing for years with no roof, no windows and no one living in them. Behind the cemetery in this book there are four of them.

**veranda** — a covered porch or passage along the front of a house, often with a low wall you can sit on. The Old Woman in this book sits on her veranda and watches everything that goes past.

**wrapper** — a large piece of cloth that a woman wraps around herself as a skirt, or over her shoulders. Also simply called "cloth." In this book, Mansa's blue wrapper is the only thing Kwame finds of hers.

**yam** — a starchy root vegetable, like a very large sweet potato, boiled or pounded into fufu. In this book Nene is paid in yam, and complains that it is not nearly enough.

**zinc** — corrugated metal sheeting, used for roofs and fences. It is called zinc in Ghana even though it is really galvanised steel.

# Part B — Difficult words in this book

Each definition has the sentence from the story, so you can see how the word is used.

**abandoned** — left somewhere and never come back for.
> *"…the body of a young woman was discovered early this morning in a vehicle abandoned near the Mallam Town cemetery road."*

**absolutely** — completely; totally; with no exception.
> *"…then dust, and quiet, and a hundred people standing absolutely still."*

**accusation** — a statement saying that somebody did something wrong.
> *"…she could not have told you herself whether it was grief or whether it was an accusation."*

**arithmetic** — working with numbers. In this book it is used to mean working out the safest thing to do.
> *"The mathematics had never once favoured pride."*

**ashamed** — feeling bad because you have done something you think is wrong.
> *"…she was ashamed of going behind her husband's back — and shame makes you careful about noise."*

**bulletin** — a short news report on the radio or television.
> *"…she heard the tail of a news bulletin, and then, at eight o'clock, the thing itself."*

**complicated** — having many parts; hard to understand or deal with.

**concussion** — an injury to the head that makes a person confused, dizzy or sleepy. It can be serious. Nene watches Kwame's pupils through the night because she is checking for it.

**contradict** — to say the opposite of what somebody else has said.
> *"One of them thinks the other one left. The other one thinks the first one left. Both of them cannot be right."*

**deliberate** — careful and unhurried; done on purpose, not by accident.
> *"Mansa Asante was thirty-eight and deliberate in every motion."*

**disbelief** — the feeling of not being able to believe what you are seeing.
> *"…he stood for a moment taking in a hundred people with the flat disbelief of a man who had asked for order and been given a town."*

**dislocated** — pulled out of its joint. A dislocated shoulder is a shoulder that has come out of its socket. Nene puts Kwame's back in.

**documented** — written down officially, so that it can be used as proof.
> *"A dislocated shoulder, set and bound. Documented on the Wednesday."*

**fishtailed** — slid sideways, with the back end swinging out. A motorcycle fishtails when it loses grip.
> *"It fishtailed at once on the loose gravel of the track."*

**immediate** — happening at once, with no delay.
> *"…Kwame Asante was cleared of all charges with immediate effect."*

**irritable** — easily annoyed; bad-tempered.

**muttered** — said something quietly and not clearly, usually because you are annoyed.

**newsreader** — the person who reads the news on the radio or television.

**official** — done or approved by somebody in authority. An official record is one the police or a hospital will accept.

**overturned** — tipped over; knocked onto its side.
> *"…an upturned bucket…"*

**potholes** — holes in the surface of a road.

**pretended** — acted as if something were true when it was not.
> Nene says she will never pretend: *"I will not pretend otherwise."*

**professional** — doing a job for money, and doing it properly. In this book the men who come for Kwame are professionals, and *"professionals do not shout."*

**pulse** — the beat of blood in your body, which you can feel at your wrist or at the side of your neck. Checking somebody's pulse is how you tell whether they are still alive. Nene checks Kwame's in the gutter: *"she put two fingers against the side of his neck and held them there, counting to herself."* Fourteen chapters later she does exactly the same thing for the man who ordered it — *"Exactly as she had done for Kwame in the gutter."*

**reluctantly** — unwillingly; doing something you do not really want to do.

**restlessness** — the feeling of not being able to keep still or settle.
> Kwabena planed the same piece of wood for three nights because of it.

**separated** — pulled apart; made to be apart.

**simmered** — cooked gently, just below boiling.
> *"A pot simmered on a single gas stove beside her."*

**suspicion** — a feeling that something is true, without being able to prove it.
> *"Suspicion is not proof."*

**unarmed** — carrying no weapon.
> *"He was unarmed. He held his hands out from his sides with the palms turned up."*

**unhurried** — not rushed; calm and slow.
> It is the word this book uses for men who are confident they will get what they came for.

**upturned** — turned over, so the bottom faces up.
> *"She sat on an upturned bucket…"*

**vibrating** — shaking, from nervousness or from holding something in too long.
> *"…while Kwame sat in the corner vibrating."*

**windowless** — having no windows.
> *"There was an inner room, windowless, with a door that locked on the outside."*

**witnesses** — people who saw something happen, and can say so.
> In this book, witnesses are the one thing Mallam Town has never provided — until it does.

# Part C — The names in this book

Most Akan names tell you the **day of the week** a person was born. Look at the cast list below and you can work out what day almost every character in this book was born on.

| Day born | Boy's name | Girl's name |
|---|---|---|
| Sunday | Kwesi, Kwasi | Akosua |
| Monday | Kojo, Kwadwo | Adwoa, Adjoa, Adzo |
| Tuesday | Kwabena | Abena |
| Wednesday | Kwaku, Kweku | Akua |
| Thursday | Yaw, Ekow | Yaa |
| Friday | Kofi | Afua, Efua |
| Saturday | Kwame | Ama |

### The characters

| Name | Who they are | What the name tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Kwame Asante** | The taxi driver, called "The Peacemaker" | Born on a **Saturday**. *Asante* is the name of Ghana's largest Akan group. |
| **Mansa Asante** | His wife | *Mansa* means **"of three"** — the Akan name for a third-born girl. |
| **Nene** | The herbalist | Not a birth name at all. A **Ga-Dangme title of respect** for a chief, a queenmother or an elder. |
| **Chief Inspector Nanfuri** | Efua's father | *Nanfuri* is a family name from northern Ghana. |
| **Inspector Adzo Mensah** | The honest inspector | *Adzo* — born on a **Monday** (the Ewe form of Adwoa). *Mensah* means **"third-born"** in Akan. |
| **Kwabena Dapaah** | The carpenter | Born on a **Tuesday**. |
| **Yaw Sarpong** | The landlord | Born on a **Thursday**. |
| **Papa Kofi** | A driver at the rank | Born on a **Friday**. "Papa" is respect, not fatherhood. |
| **Mr. Tee** | A driver at the rank | A nickname. |
| **Kwesi** | Seventeen, an apprentice | Born on a **Sunday** (the Fante form of Kwasi). |
| **Ekow** | Nineteen, an apprentice | Born on a **Thursday** (the Fante form). |
| **Maame Adjoa** | Kwesi's mother | Born on a **Monday** (the Fante form of Adwoa). |
| **Maame Akua** | Ekow's mother | Born on a **Wednesday**. |
| **Efua Nanfuri** | The young woman who died | Born on a **Friday**. |
| **Ama** | The woman at the MTN kiosk | Born on a **Saturday**. |
| **Nartey** | A man two doors from the Asantes | A Ga name. |

**Things to notice.**

Kwabena and Ekow were both born on a Thursday — but one uses the Akan name and one the Fante name. Adjoa and Adzo are the same name in two different languages. Ghana is small enough to share these names and large enough to pronounce them differently.

# Part D — Nene's medicine

### A note on the words used about Nene

Nene is a **traditional herbalist** — a healer who treats people with plants. She knows which bark to take off a tree before the sun gets hot, which leaf to pound, which paste to lay on a bruise and bind with a clean cloth. She has learned this over a lifetime, and she is very good at it.

**Words this book uses about her:**

- **herbs** — plants used as medicine
- **medicine** — what Nene makes and gives
- **her knowledge** and **her calling** — the work she was trained to do
- **the ancestors** — the dead of the community, remembered with respect

**Words this book never uses about her:**

> *fetish · juju · witch · charms · powers · spirits · magic · heathen · pagan*

This is a deliberate choice. Those words are often used wrongly and unfairly about traditional healers, and none of them are needed to tell this story. Nene says it herself, in the only conversation in this book where she and Kwame talk about God:

> **"The one who made the tree," she said, "and the one who made the leaf. You think they are different people?"**

And when she is asked to treat something that no herb can reach, she says so rather than pretending:

> **"If his head were bleeding inside," she said to the boy, "no leaf would help him. That one is for the hospital, and I will not pretend otherwise."**

**So what is Nene's power, in this book?**

Not anything supernatural. Three ordinary things:

1. **Knowledge** — forty years of it, learned leaf by leaf.
2. **Patience** — she knows when to sit and wait, and she makes Kwame learn it too.
3. **The trust of a whole town** — which is why, when she finally goes from door to door, a hundred people come out behind her.

At the end, when the man who caused all of it is lying on the track and somebody says *"It is finished for him,"* Nene is the one who answers:

Somebody says: *"It is finished for him."* Nene answers:

> **"It is not finished for him," she said. "It is beginning."**

And then:

> **"He is still a man. Somebody call the hospital."**

That is the whole point of her.
