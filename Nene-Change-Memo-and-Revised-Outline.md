# Nene the Traditional Priestess — Change Memo & Revised Outline
### Mallam Town (JHS edition) · names locked: Kwame, Mansa, Nene, Chief Inspector Nanfuri

---

## 1. Verdict

**Yes. Do this.** It's a better idea than you may realise, because it fixes a structural weakness you didn't set out to fix.

But it also carries the single biggest adoption risk in the whole project, and that risk is not a writing problem — it's a **vocabulary problem**. More on that in §3. Handle that one thing well and this becomes the strongest decision you've made about this book.

---

## 2. What the change fixes

**a) It closes the biggest credibility gap in Project-M.**
In M, a *nurse* finds a badly injured man in a gutter and takes him to her house instead of a hospital. The text has to dodge the question — *"Later, Kwame would ask her why. Her answer would be simple: 'Should I have?'"* — and it never quite worked. You can hear the author stepping around it.

With Nene, the problem evaporates. She is **the person in the community you take a broken body to.** And her not calling the police is instantly motivated: she is an old woman who has spent seventy years watching what happens to people who get handed over. No dodging required.

**b) She becomes a far better investigator.**
The nurse's advantage was *"nobody suspects a nurse asking questions."* Nene's is better: **everyone tells her things.** A traditional healer holds an enormous store of confidential knowledge. People come to her at night with what they will not tell the pastor, the imam, or the police. She would know about the two missing boys because **their mothers came to her.** That is a richer, more authentic, more Ghanaian asset than a uniform.

**c) It deletes the romantic complication at a stroke.**
M ended with Kwame pairing off with the nurse while Mansa was socially destroyed — I flagged that as the worst thing in M for a JHS text. An elderly priestess removes it entirely. No triangle, no awkward optics, and the marriage reconciliation from Projects C and G survives clean.

**d) It sharpens the theme.**
The place respectable Mallam Town is frightened of — the old woman's shrine — is the only place an innocent wanted man is safe. Meanwhile the *respectable* institutions (the police post, the compound, the landlord's house) are where the danger actually lives. That irony **is** your book's theme, made physical. It's much stronger than "a nurse's small blue room."

**e) It adds the layer M was missing.**
M's texture was good but almost entirely modern-urban: MoMo, MTN, flat-screen televisions. Nene brings the older Ghana — herbs, bark, the shrine, knowledge passed from grandmother to granddaughter. That is the country most school readers recognise from their grandparents.

**f) Her age is an asset.**
An elderly woman has watched the town's silences pile up for decades. She is the living memory of the two missing boys and the vanished construction worker. She literally *is* the town's conscience — which is what you said you wanted Mallam Town itself to be.

## 3. The three risks, and exactly how to neutralise them

### Risk 1 — Mission schools (the big one)

A large share of Ghanaian basic schools are faith-based — Catholic, Methodist, Presbyterian, Anglican, and a large and growing charismatic/Pentecostal sector. Some will object to a traditional priestess presented as wise and heroic. Traditional religion is still framed, in some church discourse, as "fetish" or demonic. If that objection lands, you lose the schools.

**The mitigation — and this is the most valuable thing in this memo:**

In Project-M, Kwame is **already a Christian**. He sits at the taxi rank with his bible open, checking the preacher's verses for accuracy. Keep that. Now place him in Nene's care and have **neither of them treat it as a problem.**

Kwame prays. Nene treats him with bark and warm water. He thanks God. She shrugs and says something like:

> *"The one who made the tree and the one who made the leaf. You think they are different people?"*

That single exchange converts your biggest liability into the book's finest moment. It is exactly what Ghana's **Religious and Moral Education** syllabus teaches about tolerance and coexistence, which means it's not a risk to a teacher — it's a *resource*. And it is unmistakably, specifically Ghanaian.

If you want to go further: Mallam has a large Zongo/Muslim population (the name itself comes from the Hausa *mālam*, an Islamic teacher). A Muslim neighbour who also respects Nene completes the picture without a single line of preaching.

**The vocabulary rule — this is what will actually decide it:**

| **Banned** | **Use instead** |
|---|---|
| fetish | shrine |
| juju | medicine, herbs |
| witch | — *(never)* |
| charms, powers | her knowledge, her calling |
| spirits, demons | the ancestors |
| heathen, pagan | traditional |
| magic | *(avoid entirely)* |

Write her as a **healer and an elder**, not as a source of mystery. The moment the prose gets misty or eerie, you lose the room.

### Risk 2 — The elderly-woman-as-witch stereotype

In Ghana, elderly women with herbal knowledge get read as *abayifoɔ* — witches. Witch accusation against older women is a real and ongoing harm in northern and southern Ghana alike. If Nene is drawn as mysterious, frightening, or supernaturally powerful, you reinforce the worst stereotype in Ghanaian popular culture — in a book aimed at children.

**Mitigation: make her domestic and specific.**

- Show her **cooking**. Show her **complaining about her knees**. Show her taking a long time to kneel down and a longer time to get up.
- Show her treating a child's fever and being paid in **yam**.
- Show her charging too little, and being told off for it.
- Show her **knowing her limits**: *"If your head is bleeding inside, no leaf will help you. That one is for the hospital, and I will not pretend otherwise."* A healer who knows what she cannot do is credible and dignified. An all-powerful one is a stereotype.
- Give her one line, in her own voice, that defuses it outright: **"My grandmother showed me the bark that brings a swelling down. That is all it is. It is not magic. It is a tree."**
- **Let the community love her.** Children run to her. Tenants bring her food. The Old Woman on the veranda defends her. Stereotypes die when you show a person being ordinary and liked.

### Risk 3 — Public-health messaging

A book for 12–15-year-olds showing a traditional healer successfully treating a concussion may read as endorsing unqualified treatment of serious injury.

**Mitigation:** keep Kwame's injuries inside her lane — **bruised ribs, a dislocated shoulder, a cut, a swollen eye.** A dislocated shoulder is perfect, because setting a joint is exactly what traditional healers are renowned for, and it's visually concrete on the page. Anything more serious, she names and declines. Let a clinic still appear positively somewhere — a child taken to it, or Nene herself saying *"that one is for the hospital."* Responsible *and* better characterisation.

## 4. A note on the name "Nene"

"Nene" is a **Ga-Dangme title** — the Ga equivalent of the Akan "Nana" — used as *"a respectful word of address to a chief, to a queenmother, or to an elder in general"* [2](https://ucp.manifoldapp.org/read/reinventing-african-chieftaincy-in-the-age-of-aids-gender-governance-and-development/section/20189904-89be-4caa-8eb4-7f28efecf32b). It denotes king, chief, or respected elder [4](https://www.names.org/n/nene/about).

**Mallam sits on Ga land in Greater Accra**, so a Ga honorific is geographically exactly right. That's a fortunate accident — keep it.

Two options:
- **Keep it as "Nene"** — reads naturally as respectful address, which is how the town would use it.
- **Give her a full form** the community uses — *Nene Korkor, Nene Dedei, Nene Adjoa, Nene Afi* — and let people address her as "Nene."

Either works. A single line locks it in and teaches it without a lecture:

> *"They call me Nene. It is not my name. It is what you call an old woman you are about to ask a favour of."*

**If you want the correct role-term in the dialogue**, depends which tradition you place her in:
- **Akan:** priests and priestesses are *akɔmfoɔ* [1](https://www.modernghana.com/ghanahome/ghana/?section=13)
- **Ga:** the high priest is a *Wulɔmɔ*; a female medium/priestess is a *wɔŋ yoo* or one of the *Woyei* [2](https://kwekudee-tripdownmemorylane.blogspot.com/2013/01/ga-people-ghanas-tribe-that-has-maintained-its-african-traditions-and-culture-in-the-midst-of-western-influence-in-the-capital-cityaccra.html)

Given Mallam is Ga, *Wulɔmɔ* is technically the high priest (usually male), while *wɔŋ yoo* is the female medium. My honest advice: **don't put the term in a 13-year-old's mouth.** Use "traditional priestess," "the old woman who knows medicine," or "Nene." Save the proper term for the glossary, where it belongs — and where a teacher will be delighted to find it.

**One setting note:** Mallam is a heavily Zongo/Muslim area, and the name itself comes from an Islamic title. Accra is genuinely plural, so Nene is plausible there. But if you want her practice to feel *rooted* rather than incidental, lean **into** the mix: make the Christian–Muslim–Traditional triangle part of the town's fabric. That's truer to Accra, and it's more interesting.

## 5. Plot mechanics that have to change

| Was (nurse) | Now (Nene) |
|---|---|
| Finds him coming off an afternoon shift | Finds him at **dawn, out gathering bark** — better motivation for being on an unlit road at 5 a.m. |
| Takes him to her small blue room | Takes him to her **shrine compound** |
| "Should I have?" (dodges the question) | **Answers it:** *"And then the police come, and they ask questions, and you are a man with bruises and no answers. I have watched that happen."* |
| A television brings the news | **The shrine brings the news.** Clients arrive with it. *That is what the shrine is — a place where the town's secrets arrive.* Better, more Ghanaian, and it makes her the information hub. |
| "Nobody suspects a nurse asking questions" | *"Nobody stops an old woman. They do not even see me. That is the whole use of me."* |
| Runs through alleys, climbs through a zinc fence | **She does not run.** See Ch. 12 — this becomes a better scene. |
| Love interest | **Elder.** Removes the entire problem. |
| — | **She knows the missing boys** because their mothers came to her. The pattern arrives through her memory, not a police file. |

**The chase scene (was M Ch. 13) now has to be rewritten** — an elderly woman cannot sprint or climb a fence. Do this instead:

> *"I am not climbing through a hole in a fence at my age. You go. I will be here when you come back, and I will say I saw nothing, and they will believe me — because who tells an old woman anything?"*

Then, when Kwame is cornered, **Nene raises the compound** and the town's people arrive, and the men melt away because there are suddenly witnesses. **The community becomes the escape.** That stages your theme instead of stating it.

**One more change I'd recommend:** move the wanted-man broadcast from national television to **local radio**. It's more authentic for Ghana, far more believable coming from a Chief Inspector, and it fits Nene's world better. *"The whole town heard it on the radio"* is a truer sentence than *"on the evening news."*

## 6. Revised outline — 14 chapters

**~10,000–12,000 words · three parts · target age 12–15**

### Part One — The Disappearance (1–4)

**1. The Peacemaker**
Dawn at the taxi rank. Kwame settles a quarrel between two drivers (use the "blue shirt" version — it shows his gift rather than just his knowledge). That evening: Mansa, the argument about the MoMo balance, and Kwame walking out at 11:42 p.m. The compound gate does not scream. **Plant Nene early** — Kwame greets her on the dark road, or we see children running to her door.

**2. The Empty Room**
He comes back for his phone. Perfume. No Mansa. Two rejected calls.

**3. Dangerous Lane**
The carjacking: two men and a woman. *"Not the face. Leave his hands."* Bruised ribs, a dislocated shoulder, a cut above the eye.

**4. Nene**
Dawn. She finds him in the gutter, out gathering bark. **Show the treatment in full** — the shoulder going back in, the bark, the warm cloth. Show her limits. Show Kwame asking why she didn't take him to the hospital, and give her the real answer: *"And then the police come and ask questions, and you are a man with bruises and no answers. I have watched that happen. I am not watching it again."*

### Part Two — The Lie (5–8)

**5. The Report**
Kwame insists on going anyway. **Chief Inspector Nanfuri** — brisk, tired, grief already in him and we don't yet know why. Mansa reported missing, taxi reported stolen. It gets logged. Nobody looks. Nene was right. Let that land as a small, satisfying beat.

**6. A Face on the Radio**
News: **Efua Nanfuri**, twenty-four, found dead in a stolen taxi. Kwame's taxi. Then Kwame's own face, named a murderer. The news reaches Nene first through a client — because that is what her house is.

**7. What Nene Knows**
Nene tells him what she has heard over the years: the two boys who went missing, the construction worker who vanished near the uncompleted houses behind the cemetery, the mothers who came to her for medicine for children who never came back to thank her. **The pattern arrives through her memory.** This is the chapter where she becomes the town's conscience.

**8. Mansa**
Mansa is alive, held near the uncompleted houses. She hears the news and believes it. She does not curse him — she says on the radio that she no longer knows the man she married. Keep it painful, keep it non-spiritual.

### Part Three — The Truth (9–14)

**9. What Mansa Saw**
The night delivery at the uncompleted houses. She recognised one of the men. She told the wrong person.

**10. The Plan**
**Nene proposes it, not Kwame.** Keep the agency line — it is still the best line in the book, and it is *better* in an old woman's mouth:
> *"You are not asking me. I am offering. There is a difference — and at my age I have earned the right to decide."*

**11. The Carpenter**
Kofi Dapaah comes to Nene's to be paid for a job. Sees Kwame. Freezes. Comes back later: *"I built the door."* (The bolt-hole into the uncompleted house — not a peephole.)

**12. The Narrow Escape**
Kwame goes to the uncompleted houses. The men nearly corner him. He runs; **Nene does not.** She raises the compound. The town arrives. The men melt away because there are now witnesses.

**13. The Honest Inspector**
**Inspector Mensah** — one rank below Nanfuri, so the hierarchy reads correctly — comes to Nene's door not to arrest but to ask. He has the file; she has the memory. Between them, the picture completes.

**14. Dawn at the Uncompleted House**
Public reckoning in front of the compound and the town. Mansa freed. Kwame cleared. Chief Inspector Nanfuri — who branded him — has to face him. Then Kwame's line: *"I spent my whole life thinking peace meant keeping quiet."* The gate at the end of the rank screams, and this time everybody hears. **Final beat: Nene doing something ordinary** — sweeping, or scolding a child. Because that is the point of her.

## 7. Sample prose — opening of Chapter 4

> She found him at first light, because she was always up at first light. The bark she wanted came off the tree behind the cemetery, and it came off easier before the sun had properly thought about it.
>
> Nene was not her name, not really. Nene was what you called an old woman you were about to ask a favour of.
>
> He was face-down in the gutter on Dangerous Lane with one arm underneath him at a wrong angle. She had set shoulders before. Her nephew's, the year he fell out of the mango tree. Two of the market women's. One belonging to a man who never thanked her and never would.
>
> She knelt, which took her a while and hurt, and turned him over by the good arm.
>
> "Ei," she said. "Who did this to you?"
>
> He did not answer. His eye was shut, his breathing shallow, and there was dust in his mouth.
>
> She did the shoulder first, because a shoulder out of its socket will not wait, and because the rest — the ribs, the cut, the eye — was the sort of thing a body heals on its own if nobody interferes. She had learnt that from her grandmother, who learnt it from hers, and none of them had ever called it anything but work.
>
> The shoulder went back with a sound that made her own bones ache in sympathy.
>
> Then she sat back on her heels and looked at him properly, and saw what she had missed in the half-dark. This was Kwame. The taxi driver. The Peacemaker. The one they all said never raised his voice.
>
> "So," Nene said to the empty road. "Even you."

## 9. Also worth noting

**"Chief Inspector Nanfuri" fixes an authenticity problem I flagged in my first report.** "Commander" is not a Ghana Police Service rank (it's UK/Metropolitan); the GPS runs Inspector → Chief Inspector → ASP → DSP → Superintendent. A Chief Inspector heading a **police post** is exactly right. Good call — and it means the honest investigator should sit *below* him, hence Inspector Mensah.

**New glossary entries you'll need:** shrine · traditional priestess · herbs/medicine · libation · the ancestors · *Nene* · *Wulɔmɔ* / *akɔmfoɔ* (if you use them). Write them in the same plain, respectful register as your existing "compound house" entry — that entry is the model. And remember the glossary currently says *"For SHS Literature students"*; that needs to change to match your JHS decision.
