Reading Test Kit

Sheet 1 of 8 · for the teacher

What this is for. This tests the book on real pupils before you spend money printing it. It answers three questions, and only three: can they read it? do they understand it? do they want to keep going? Nothing else in this kit matters as much as those three.

Who to test

Eight to ten pupils. Do not test only your best readers — they will tell you the book is easy, and they are not the pupils the book was written for. Pick two strong readers, four or five in the middle, and two or three who struggle. If you can, include at least two JHS 1 pupils: if the book works for the bottom of JHS 1, it works for the whole school.

How to run it

  1. One pupil at a time. Never in front of the class. A child who is ashamed reads worse than they are, and you will get a false answer.
  2. Give them Sheet 2. Do not pre-teach it. Do not explain the story, do not read the title aloud, do not define trotro or kenkey first. The question is what the page does on its own.
  3. Say only this: “Read this out loud to me. Read it the way you would read it to yourself. I am not going to help you while you read — but if a word beats you, say the word ‘pass’ and go on. When you finish I will ask you about it.”
  4. Time it. Phone stopwatch, from their first word. Stop when they reach the end. Write the seconds on Sheet 5.
  5. Mark miscues as they read on your own copy of Sheet 2 — do not wait, you will forget. Use the marks on Sheet 5.
  6. Do not correct them while reading. If they self-correct, credit them. If they ask for a word, tell it to them and mark it — then count it as an error.
  7. Then ask the six questions on Sheet 4. Ask them in order, and ask the last one even if the pupil is clearly struggling. Write the answer in your own words; you are judging understanding, not memory of the exact phrasing.
  8. Hand them Sheet 7 — the slip. Anonymous. Collect them all before you read any.

What not to do

  • Do not tell them it is your book.
  • Do not stop them to correct pronunciation mid-sentence.
  • Do not run it as a comprehension test they write — this is an oral reading test, and writing fluency is a different skill that will contaminate your result.
  • Do not test more than about six pupils in one session. Your ear goes dull.

Total time: about ten minutes per pupil. Two passages, so roughly fifteen minutes if you do both. Passage A is the cold open — the page a child would judge the book by in a bookshop. Passage B is the hardest scene in the book, the one carrying the most inference. If the book is going to fail, it will fail on B.

Passage A

Sheet 2 of 8 · for the pupil · 433 words

Chapter One — The Peacemaker · opening

Mallam Town woke up at four and did not wait for anybody.

By seven the taxi rank was already full of dust and shouting and horns. Old yellow taxis nosed in one after another, their paint faded by the sun. Drivers leaned against bonnets arguing about fuel prices. A kenkey seller went past calling out her prices. At the corner a preacher's public address system coughed into life, far too early, and a hundred people pretended not to hear it.

Kwame Asante sat on an overturned plastic crate beside his own taxi, polishing his side mirror with a rag. The rag was far cleaner than the car deserved.

When the preacher got going, Kwame listened for a while, and then opened the bible on his knee to check the verse. He did this most mornings. Not to argue — to see whether the man was telling the truth. If he found that he was not, Kwame simply stopped listening. He had never once said anything to anybody about it.

He was forty-four, tall and lanky, with quiet hands and a face that did not hurry to show what he was thinking. Twenty years at this rank had taught him that most arguments are not about what they say they are about, and that nearly all of them go quiet on their own if nobody feeds them.

Peace, in Kwame's life, meant keeping quiet long enough for other people's noise to die down.

He did not yet know how much that belief was going to cost him.

At the other end of the rank, Papa Kofi and Mr. Tee were arguing again.

"You are telling me the man paid you for three?" Mr. Tee said.

"He paid me for two and walked away on the third," Papa Kofi said. "So who is the thief here?"

They had been at it since six. The argument had started somewhere else entirely and had only now found its way to the station.

Kwame did not look up from his mirror.

"It was not my customer," Mr. Tee said.

"It was yours."

"It was not."

Kwame waited until they had both run out of words. Then he said, without raising his voice:

"Was the man wearing a blue shirt?"

Mr. Tee turned. "Yes —"

"Then that is the one Papa Kofi dropped at the junction an hour ago. Not yours."

A beat.

Mr. Tee opened his mouth. Closed it again. He muttered something about the price of fuel and walked off to his car.

Papa Kofi grinned and clapped Kwame on the shoulder.

"Peacemaker," he said. "Every time."

Passage B

Sheet 3 of 8 · for the pupil · 382 words

Chapter Nine — What Mansa Saw · the broadcast

It was two mornings after the first broadcast. The radio was on. Kwame was not listening.

He had learned not to listen. The news came round every hour with his own name in it, and after a while a man stops hearing his own name, the way you stop hearing a generator.

Then a voice came out of the radio that he knew.

"Relatives of the suspect have also come forward. In a statement released this morning, the wife of Kwame Asante says the couple separated following a disagreement, and that she left the compound of her own choice. She says she does not wish to be found. In the recording she says: 'I no longer know the man I married.'"

Kwame was on his feet before he knew he had stood up.

Nene reached over and turned the radio up.

"— the recording was made available to stations this morning —"

"Play it again," Kwame said.

"That is the news. They do not play it again because you ask them to."

"Turn it up."

They waited through fuel prices, and an advertisement for a funeral home, and a phone-in about the state of the road to Weija. Then it came round again, and Kwame heard his wife say the words in his own language, in the flat voice of somebody reading from a page.

And he understood two things, one after the other.

The first was: she is alive.

He had not known he had been carrying the other thing. He had not let himself think it. But it had been there under everything since the second night, and now it lifted, and he put his hand flat on the wall to steady himself.

The second was: she said she left.

"She said she left," Kwame said. "Of her own choice. She said she does not wish to be found."

"That is what she said."

"I came back." He turned to her. "I walked out and I came back ten minutes later and the room was empty and her perfume was still in the air, and I called her twice and —"

"I know."

"She thinks I walked out on her."

"Yes."

Kwame stood with that. Outside, a trotro went past with its horn going. The radio moved on to sport.

The hardest passage in the book. Test it second, and only with pupils who managed Passage A.

Comprehension Questions

Sheet 4 of 8 · for the teacher

Passage A — six questions

1–4 are literal: the answer is on the page. 5–6 are inferential: the answer is not written down. The inferential ones are the real test — a child can decode every word on the page and still not be reading it.

  1. What time does Mallam Town wake up?
  2. What is Kwame doing while Papa Kofi and Mr. Tee argue?
  3. Why does Kwame open his bible when the preacher is speaking?
  4. What one question does Kwame ask that ends the argument?
  5. Why do the other drivers call Kwame “the Peacemaker”?
  6. The page says his belief “was going to cost him.” What do you think is going to happen to him?

Passage B — six questions

  1. Why had Kwame stopped listening to the radio?
  2. What does Nene do the moment Kwame stands up?
  3. What are the two things Kwame understands, one after the other?
  4. Why does Kwame put his hand flat on the wall?
  5. Nene says “both of them cannot be right.” What does she mean?
  6. Nene says this has happened twice, so it is not an accident. What does she think it is?

Scoring

  • 5 or 6 correct — independent. The pupil understood it alone.
  • 4 correct — instructional. Understood most of it. This is the target for the middle of your class.
  • 3 or fewer — frustration. Too hard.

Credit an answer that is right in substance even if the wording is clumsy. Do not penalise pronunciation. Do not accept an answer that only repeats words from the question.

Glossary Check

Sheet 6 of 8 · for the teacher

A glossary is only useful if a twelve-year-old can actually use it alone. Most school glossaries fail this test, because nobody ever checks.

How

  1. Photocopy the glossary page from the manuscript. Give it to the pupil. Say nothing else.
  2. Ask: “Find me trotro.” Time them. Then “Find me kenkey.” Then “What is a compound?”
  3. Watch what they do. Do they scan alphabetically? Do they give up? Do they go to Part A or start at the top and read down?
PupilFound “trotro”SecondsFound “kenkey”SecondsExplained “compound”?

Pass: every pupil finds all three in under 30 seconds each, without help, and can say what compound means in their own words. Fail: any pupil gives up, or finds the word but cannot say what it means. A glossary that is found but not understood is decoration.

If it fails

  • They cannot find it — the entries are too dense for the page. Add more space between entries, or set headwords in bold so the eye can catch them while scanning.
  • They find it but cannot explain it — the definition is written for adults. Rewrite it from the sentence the word appears in, in the book.
  • They look in Part B for a Ghanaian word — the split between “words from Ghana” and “difficult words” is not obvious to a child. Say it louder on the page.

Question 4 is the important one and the one adults never think to ask. These pupils live in this world. If the town on the page is not the town outside the window, they will tell you — and that is a defect no readability formula will ever catch.

What The Numbers Mean

Sheet 8 of 8 · for the teacher

The decision

What you findWhat it meansWhat to do
The middle pupil reads at instructional level or better, and 7 of 10 score 4/6 or more The book is right.Go to print. Do not fiddle with it further.
The middle pupil reads it, but several score 3/6 or less The words are beatable; the meaning is not. Do not simplify the vocabulary. Add bridging sentences — one plain sentence after each hard passage saying what just happened.
Strong readers fine, middle pupils stumble on every page Too hard. The book is set above the class. See the remedies below. Retest before printing.
Everyone reads it easily, nobody wants the next chapter Too easy, or too flat. Unlikely with this book, but if it happens the problem is pacing, not reading level. Look at Chapters 5 and 6.

Diagnostics — let the errors tell you what to fix

Do not read the miscues as one number. Read where they cluster. The cluster tells you the remedy.

Where the errors clusterThe actual problemThe fix
Ghanaian words — trotro, kenkey, compound, okada Vocabulary, not difficulty Introduce the word in a sentence before it matters, or let the glossary carry it. Never remove the word — it is the pupil's own world.
Long sentences with commas Sentence length, not word difficulty Split at the comma. Two short sentences beat one correct one, every time.
Passage B but not Passage A Inference load. The pupil is decoding fine and not understanding Add a sentence making the unspoken thing spoken. Nene's reasoning is the usual culprit.
Names — Kwabena, Nanfuri, Sarpong Too many similar names at once Do not rename them. Space out the introductions, and bring back the cast list at the front.
Scattered evenly, everywhere The book is simply set too high This is a rewrite, not a patch. Reconsider the whole register.

One caution that is easy to get wrong. A pupil reading slowly is not the same as a pupil reading badly. A child who stops at a hard sentence because they are thinking about it is doing exactly what the book wants. Watch their face before you look at your stopwatch. If they are frowning in concentration rather than guessing at words, leave them alone and leave the book alone.

After the test

Keep the slips and the clocking sheets. When you next sit down with an illustrator or a printer, the single most useful thing you can hand them is evidence from the pupils the book is for. It is also the thing that settles an argument fastest.
