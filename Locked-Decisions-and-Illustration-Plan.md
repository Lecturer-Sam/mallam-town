## 2. Flag 1 — "Kofi Papa" collides with Kofi Dapaah

**Kofi Dapaah is already the carpenter** — the man who says *"I built the door."* Two major characters called Kofi in a 12,000-word book written for 12–15-year-olds will trip readers constantly, and it will trip you too. In Project-M, exactly this kind of near-duplicate name ("Wan Driver" leaking into "Commander Wan Driver Nanfuri") produced real errors that are still in the text.

**My recommendation: rename the carpenter, keep your new driver name.** His surname matters more than his day-name, and *"Dapaah"* carries the line.

- **Kwabena Dapaah** — born on Tuesday *(my pick)*
- **Yaw Dapaah** — born on Thursday
- **Kodwo Dapaah** — born on Monday

**Secondary note:** "Kofi Papa" and "Mr. Tee" are in different registers — one is an Akan day-name, one is an English nickname. That's actually fine, because **Ghanaian drivers really do go by nicknames**, and the rank is exactly where nicknames live. But if you want them to read as a pair, style the first as **"Papa Kofi"** — the natural Ghanaian form of respect for an older man — against "Mr. Tee." Two nicknames, same register, instantly memorable.

## 3. Flag 2 — "Adzo" reads feminine. Keep it, and make Mensah a woman.

**Adzo** is an Ewe day-name, traditionally given to a girl born on Monday. As a name for a male inspector, most Ghanaian readers will stumble over it.

**My recommendation: don't change the name — change the character. Make Inspector Adzo Mensah a woman.**

This improves the book in four ways:

1. **It balances the authority in the story.** The male authority figure (Chief Inspector Nanfuri) is the one who gets it catastrophically wrong and has to be corrected. The person who quietly gets it right is a woman. That's a clean, teachable contrast that costs you nothing.
2. **You get three women across three generations** — Nene the elder, Inspector Mensah the professional, Mansa the survivor. Each of them acts; none of them is rescued.
3. **It sharpens Ch. 13.** A young female inspector walking alone into an old herbalist's compound at night to ask for help, against her own Chief Inspector's orders, is a far better scene than the same beat with a man.
4. **It's realistic.** The Ghana Police Service has plenty of women at that rank.

If you'd rather keep Mensah male, the masculine forms are **Kodzo** or **Kojo** (Monday-born) — but I'd genuinely urge you toward Adzo Mensah as a woman.

## 4. Sarpong's fate — keep the okada, and let Nene carry it

### Why the okada is a good instinct

It's unmistakably Ghanaian, a 13-year-old can see it in one frame, and it's **visual rather than verbal** — you can stage it almost without dialogue. It also gives Sarpong desperation and agency instead of having him stand there and be arrested.

### The one risk: permanent paralysis as the moral payoff

Two problems for a school text:

- **Graphic injury.** "Lower body run over by a car" is visceral. At 12–15 that needs real restraint.
- **Disability as punishment.** If the book's closing note on the villain is *he is now crippled*, that reads as "this is what a bad man deserves," which frames disability as a fitting penalty. Ghana's inclusive-education policy and any disability-aware reviewer will flag it. It also undercuts the moral care you've taken everywhere else — we deliberately removed the shunning-as-justice ending from Project-M for exactly this reason.

### The recommended handling — four rules

1. **Nobody touches him.** He does this to himself. The crowd parts and lets him through because there is nowhere left to go. **No mob, no stones, no hands.** This is what keeps the town on the right side of the line.
2. **Write the sound, not the body.** Not what the car did to him — the noise, the dust, and the faces of the people watching.
3. **Give Nene the moral beat.** She is a healer; of course she is the one who goes to him. And she is the one who refuses to call it justice.
4. **Let the hospital be the ending of that thread,** not the roadside. Somebody calls an ambulance. That is what a town that has learned to look does.

### Sample — written to show the restraint I mean

> The okada was already running. He had left it at the far corner of the third house with the engine idling, the way a man leaves a thing when he is not planning to stay long.
>
> He saw the door standing open. He saw the cement and the wire and the stripped panels of a taxi with a dent in the rear passenger door. He saw Mansa Asante standing in the daylight, blinking, with somebody's coat over her shoulders.
>
> He turned and ran.
>
> Nobody followed him. That was the thing Kwame remembered afterwards — that not one person laid a hand on him. The crowd simply parted, the way a crowd does, and let him through, because there was nowhere left for him to go.
>
> He got the bike moving. It fishtailed on the loose gravel of the track — the track he had never had surfaced, because a surfaced track is a track people remember — and went down hard on its side, and Sarpong went with it.
>
> The car coming up behind him was not going fast. It did not matter.
>
> The sound was not a scream. It was a noise Kwame had heard once years ago at the rank and had never managed to forget: the ordinary, muffled sound of a body in the way of something heavy.
>
> Then dust, and silence, and every single person in that crowd standing absolutely still.
>
> Nene was the first to move. She went forward leaning on her stick, and knelt — which took her a while, and hurt — and put two fingers at the side of his neck, the way she had done for Kwame in the gutter.
>
> Somebody behind her said it, breathless and half-pleased: *"It is finished for him."*
>
> Nene did not look up.
>
> *"It is not finished for him,"* she said. *"It is beginning. He is still a man. Somebody call the hospital."*

*(Note the two callbacks doing quiet work: she checks the pulse exactly as she did for Kwame in Ch. 4, and *"that one is for the hospital"* — her refrain from Ch. 4 — comes back as mercy instead of a referral. Her knowing her limits is what saved Kwame in Ch. 13. Here it saves the man who ruined him.)*

### Structural consequence: new Chapter 15

The crash is too big to bolt onto the end of Ch. 14 without derailing Mansa's truth and Nanfuri's apology. Split it:

| Ch | Title | Words |
|---|---|---|
| 14 | **Dawn at the Uncompleted House** — the walk, the proof, Mansa out, Mansa's truth, Nanfuri's apology, *"Not anymore"* | 1,100 |
| 15 | **The Okada** — Sarpong bolts, the crash, Nene, the walk back, Nene ordinary, **the gate screams** | 700 |
| | **New total** | **12,400** |

A short, quiet final chapter after a loud one is a good rhythm for this age group. And it protects your best image: the gate screaming, and everybody hearing.

## 5. Illustration plan — 15 drawings

> **Superseded.** The full commissioning document is now **`Mallam-Town-Illustration-Brief.md`**. Use that. (Corrections it makes to the list below: the carpenter is **Kwabena Dapaah**, not Kofi; Chapter 12 uses **phone lights**, not torches; Mansa does **not** mark days on the wall — she watches the square of window-light.)


**Style: black-and-white line art.** Not shaded, not tonal. Line art reproduces cheaply on the paper Ghanaian textbooks are printed on, it survives bad photocopying, and it suits a 70–80 page reader.

| 3 | **Dangerous Lane** — the taxi blocked front and back; three figures approaching, unhurried. Headlights in dust. |

| 8 | **Mansa** — Mansa in the dark room; one rectangle of light under the door; her hand marking days on the wall. |

| 14 | **Dawn at the Uncompleted House** — Kofi's hands on the door in the wall; the crowd behind him; dawn light coming in. |
| 15 | **The Gate** — Kwame pushing through the gate at the end of the rank, with Mallam Town waking behind him. **Warm, ordinary, resolving.** |

### Four production notes

1. **Fix the character designs first.** One sheet: Kwame's taxi, Nene's stick and basket, Mansa's wrapper, Inspector Mensah's cap, Kofi's hands. Young readers track characters by silhouette, and consistency is what makes a reader feel professional.
2. **Commission a map for the endpapers** — the taxi rank, Kwame's compound, Dangerous Lane, Nene's compound, the cemetery, the uncompleted houses, the police post. Cheap to produce, hugely valuable in a school reader, and it makes the geography of the chase legible. **This is the single best-value addition on this list.**
3. **A hard rule for the illustrator on Nene: no masks, no skulls, no shadows, no eeriness.** She is drawn as what she is — an old woman with bad knees and a basket. Given the stereotype risk we discussed, this is not a preference; it's a requirement. Brief it in writing.
4. **Cover:** Kwame's taxi at dawn on the empty road, or the compound gate half open. Avoid a running man with a gun — this is a book about a town that learns to look, not an action thriller.
