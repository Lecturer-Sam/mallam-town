# M / C / G — A Comparison for the JHS Decision

**Three documents, same premise, three different stages of work.**
- **Project-M** — *Mallam Town: A Novel*. 50pp, **15,952 words of prose** + glossary + study notes. A finished draft.
- **Project-C** — 4pp, **1,113 words**. A **24-beat treatment**. Ends with three undecided premise questions.
- **Project-G** — 3pp, **773 words**. A **16-beat synopsis**. Resolved, with a beginning, middle and end.

---

## 1. The first thing to say

**M is a book. C and G are blueprints for a book.**

C and G are bullet-point outlines — no scenes, no dialogue, no sentences on the page. So most of what I praised in M (the prose rhythm, the Ghanaian texture, the motifs, the voice) simply doesn't exist in C or G yet, and neither do most of the problems. Comparing them fairly means comparing **the story each one promises**, not the writing.

Which means the honest answer to "are these better suited for JHS students?" is:

> **Yes and no. C and G fix the moral problems. They introduce new ones, and they throw away your best material.**

Let me show you where each of those is true.

## 3. What C and G have in common

Beats 1–12 are **identical** in both files (I diffed them — ten are word-for-word, two differ only by line breaks). They diverge at beat 13:

- **C beat 13 onward:** *The Chief's Wrath* → Efua's Secret → Mansa's Doubt → **The Carpenter's Price** (blackmail) → The Trap Closes → The Man Behind the Robbery → Two Paths Converge → **The Formal Accusation** (forged confession, doctored CCTV) → Reunion → Alliance → Showdown → Redemption.
- **G beat 13 onward:** *The Traitor Unmasked* (undercover detective) → The Convergence → The Confrontation → Vindication and Rebirth.

Both also carry the same unresolved header: *"Mystery → Survival → Revelation → Accusation → Alliance → **\*\*\***"* — the final act is literally unnamed in both.

And both beats 1–12 are a compressed version of M's Part One. So what you've actually done is: **take M's first half, and write two different second halves for it.**

---

## 4. The one change that matters most: Mansa

This is the whole ballgame, and your instinct is right.

**In M, Mansa is the villain.** She planned her departure, emptied the MoMo account, let the compound believe he abandoned her, and was *bored* about it — *"that's not my business, Kwame."* It's brilliant, sophisticated, and completely unsuitable for 13-year-olds. On top of that, M ends with **Kwame pairing off with the nurse while his wife is socially destroyed by the whole town.** For a school text, that's a bigger problem than the peephole: it rewards the hero with a new woman and punishes the wife by communal shunning. No JHS adoption committee signs off on that.

**In C and G, Mansa is a victim** and the marriage is rebuilt. That single change removes, in one move:

- the adultery
- the wife-as-antagonist
- the community-shunning-as-justice ending
- the hero-ends-up-with-another-woman problem

**This is the right call.** Keep it.

---

## 5. Where C and G are genuinely better for JHS

1. **No sexual content at all.** No voyeuristic peephole, no landlord propositioning tenants, no honeytrap. You don't have to perform surgery on Ch. 9–11 the way M requires — the problem simply isn't there.
2. **Moral clarity.** Wrongly accused good man, loyal helpers, exposed villains, restored marriage. A 13-year-old always knows who to root for. M's Mansa deliberately blurs that, which is sophisticated but not JHS.
3. **Forgiveness is the ending.** *"Rebuilding broken trust"* is a far better final note for a school reader than *"a town too small to let her start over."*
4. **Marriage is treated as worth saving.** Enormously important for the Ghanaian school market.
5. **The hero stays heroic.** In M, Kwame hides in a woman's house for three weeks while his wife is pilloried. In C/G he actively pursues the truth.

## 6. Where C and G are worse — and this is the important half

### (a) They are *conceptually harder*, even though they're *morally simpler*

I measured it:

| | Flesch–Kincaid | Gunning Fog | Polysyllables |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M** | **5.1** | **6.9** | **7.9%** |
| C | 9.8 | 12.1 | 13.3% |
| G | 10.1 | 12.6 | 14.9% |

Now, part of that is an artifact of bullet-point writing (no easy dialogue to pull the average down). But read the vocabulary: *"orchestrated diversion"*, *"high-profile smuggling syndicate"*, *"leverage"*, *"faction"*, *"in absentia"*, *"manufactured evidence"*, *"doctored CCTV clip"*, *"tactical unit"*, *"pawns"*, *"estranged"*, *"vindication"*, *"neutralize the threat"*.

**Here's the trap:** M's story is *domestic and concrete* — a wife, a landlord, a gate, a phone, a smell. A 12-year-old can hold it in their head. C and G's story is *institutional and abstract* — syndicates, ledgers, dirty money flowing into officers' pockets, corrupt factions, undercover detectives. A 12-year-old cannot picture "dirty money flowing into high-ranking officers' pockets." They can absolutely picture a landlord who knows too much.

**You have made the book safer by making it less intimate — and less intimate is harder to read at 13.**

### (b) You've thrown away your best asset

Everything that made M worth publishing is gone from C and G:

- **Mallam Town itself** — the taxi rank, the compound house, the preachers with PA systems, the kenkey seller, FanIce, the assemblyman's unkept promise of streetlights, the MTN shop. That texture is your signature; a harbour syndicate could be anywhere in the world.
- **The theme.** M's "Peacemaker" arc — *peace-as-silence → peace-as-truth*, ending on **"Not anymore"** — is the single most teachable thing you've written. C and G keep the nickname ("popularly called 'The Peacemaker'") but as a label, not an irony. **That's the difference between a plot and a theme.** For a school text, the theme is the product. Right now C and G have no theme.
- **The motifs.** 11:42 p.m. The gate that did not scream. Perfume in an empty room. The warm phone. "Okay." Hands — *"Not the face. Leave his hands."* All gone.
- **The social conscience.** M's best idea: two boys vanished and nobody looked, but a commander's daughter dies and the whole system moves. That's a genuinely powerful, teachable critique. G replaces it with generic police corruption, which is both less original and less pointed.

### (c) Abena loses her agency

In M she says *"You're not asking. I'm offering. There's a difference."* — the best line in the book. In G she ends as *"the courageous nurse who chose compassion over suspicion"*, and Kwame and Mansa are *"forever indebted"* to her. She's been demoted from partner to helpful plot device. **Don't do this** — her agency is one of the few things that makes the book modern.

### (d) G's climax is a *deus ex machina*

> *"the nurse broadcasts a live audio recording of the conspiracy over local emergency channels using the detective's police radio, freezing the tactical unit in place."*

Three problems: it's technically complicated for a 13-year-old, it's not very believable, and it resolves the story with a gadget rather than with character. Compare M's ending — Kwame standing in front of the cameras and saying *"I spent most of my life thinking peace meant staying quiet. I don't believe that anymore."* That's a climax earned by who he is.

### (e) Two plot holes in G

- **Why would a police chief's daughter have ledger files?** She needs a job that plausibly puts her near them — bank clerk, accountant, journalist, intern at the ports authority. As written, they just fall into her hands.
- **What is Mansa's work?** She "stumbled upon" a harbour smuggling syndicate "during her own work" — but C and G never say what that work is. In M she's a wife at home. You need to define it.

### (f) Two content flags of your own

- **"She believes the news, and curses Kwame"** (beat 7, in both). "Curses" is culturally loaded in Ghana — it can read as invoking harm, not mere denouncing. Some church-affiliated schools will object to a wife cursing her husband. Consider *"denounces him on television"* or *"says on television that she no longer knows the man she married."* Same pain, no objection.
- **"call his bluff, or find a way to neutralize the threat"** (C, beat 16). "Neutralize" implies violence. In a book where the hero is a role model, that verb will read badly. Rewrite.

### (g) A naming collision

| | **M** | **C** | **G** |
|---|---|---|---|
| The nurse | **Abena** | **Efua** | *"the nurse"* (unnamed) |
| The chief's dead daughter | **Efua Nanfuri** | *(unnamed)* | *(unnamed)* |
| The police chief | Commander **Nanfuri** | Chief **Boateng** | *"the police chief"* |
| The carpenter | Kofi Dapaah | *"the carpenter"* | *"the carpenter"* |

**In C you've named the nurse "Efua" — but in M, Efua is the murdered daughter.** Pick one scheme and hold it. My suggestion: **Efua = the chief's daughter** (it's the stronger, more moving use of the name) and **Abena = the nurse**. Also settle the chief's surname — Nanfuri or Boateng, not both.

### (h) C is not finished

C ends with three questions **you** are asking yourself:

> *Who ordered the robbery, and why? · Why did Mansa disappear? · Does Chief Boateng redeem himself, stay an antagonist, or turn out to be complicit?*

Until those are answered, C isn't a story — it's a list of possibilities. And at 24 beats it's simply **too much plot for a JHS reader**: carpenter blackmail, forged confessions, doctored CCTV, a nurse with a secret, Mansa running a parallel investigation, a formal charge *in absentia*. That's an adult thriller. G's 16 beats, one throughline, is the right scale.

---

## 7. Verdict

**G over C.** It's resolved, it's half the length, it has one clean spine (conspiracy → trap → public vindication), and it makes the same moral improvements as C without the sprawl. C is worth keeping as a **sequel** or an SHS version — it has good material in it (the forged confession is a strong beat) but it is not a JHS book.

**But neither G nor C is yet a better book than M.** They're a better *moral architecture* wrapped around a weaker *story*, set in a thinner world, with no theme.

**The real answer is a synthesis: G's morals in M's world, at M's reading level.**

---

## 8. The synthesis — what I'd actually build

Take **M's setting, characters, texture and theme**; adopt **G's moral structure** (Mansa innocent, marriage restored, no sexual content); write it at **10,000–12,000 words** (~70–80pp at JHS type size) in **M's plain, short-sentence style**.

**Keep from M:** Mallam Town · the taxi rank · the compound house · MoMo · kenkey/FanIce/trotro/chop bar · the 11:42 timestamp · the gate that does not scream · the perfume · *"Not the face. Leave his hands"* · Kofi Dapaah the carpenter · Inspector Mensah as the honest cop · the two missing boys · **the "Peacemaker" arc → "Not anymore"** · Abena's agency.

**Change from M:** Mansa is taken, not absconded · no peephole · no affair · no honeytrap · the nurse does not end up with the husband · public vindication instead of a shootout.

**Why Mansa was taken (keeps it local, drops the harbour):** she saw the men unloading goods at night into the **uncompleted houses behind the cemetery** — the same houses M already uses — and she told the wrong person. That keeps the crime three streets from the hero's bedroom, keeps your missing-boys motif, and gives Kofi Dapaah something to have built and to feel guilty about.

**Proposed shape — 14 chapters, three parts:**

**Part One – The Disappearance (1–4)**
1. *The Peacemaker* — dawn at the rank; Kwame settles a quarrel; the MoMo argument; he walks out at 11:42; the gate that does not scream.
2. *The Empty Room* — perfume, no Mansa, rejected calls.
3. *Dangerous Lane* — the carjacking.
4. *The Nurse* — Abena finds him and takes him home.

**Part Two – The Lie (5–8)**
5. *The Report* — the police post logs it and forgets it. Meet **Inspector Mensah** here, by name.
6. *A Face on the Television* — Efua is dead in his taxi; Kwame is wanted.
7. *The Carpenter* — Kofi returns for his payment, sees Kwame, freezes. *"I built the door."* (Now a **bolt-hole into the uncompleted house**, not a peephole — same chill, none of the risk.)
8. *Mansa* — she is alive, and afraid. She hears the news and believes it. She **denounces** him, not curses him.

**Part Three – The Truth (9–14)**
9. *What Mansa Saw* — the night delivery; the wrong person told.
10. *The Plan* — Abena proposes it (her idea, her risk, her choice).
11. *The Narrow Escape* — Kwame leaves the house; the chase through the alleys and the uncompleted houses.
12. *The Honest Cop* — Mensah brings the pattern: the two boys, the construction worker, Efua.
13. *Dawn at the Uncompleted House* — the truth said out loud in front of the whole compound. No tactical units, no police radio. **The town is the witness** — which pays off your best idea, that Mallam Town itself is a character.
14. *Not Anymore* — Kwame cleared; Mansa and Kwame begin again honestly; Peacemaker redefined; **the gate screams, and this time everybody hears.**

That book is: readable at 12, clean of every objection, set in a world only you could write, and built on a theme an examiner can set an essay on.

---

## 9. Five things to decide before you write

1. **Age band** — JHS 2–3 (12–15)? Fix it and put it on the copyright page.
2. **Names** — Efua = the chief's daughter, Abena = the nurse, one surname for the chief. Lock it in a character bible.
3. **Mansa's job and Efua's job** — both need occupations that make the plot possible.
4. **Who the villain is** — I'd keep a local one (the landlord as the receiver) rather than a corrupt police faction. A child can understand a greedy landlord; "a faction of the police" is harder and more contentious in a school book.
5. **Length and format** — 10–12k words, 14 short chapters, larger type, one illustration per chapter if you can. Plus a glossary and 8–10 discussion questions.
