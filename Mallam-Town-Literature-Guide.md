# MALLAM TOWN — Literature Study Guide
### by Lecturer Sam · for JHS English Language (Literature)

---

## How to use this guide

This guide takes the novel apart in fifteen ways. Each section does three things:

1. **What it means** — the term, explained plainly, so you can use it in an exam answer
2. **In *Mallam Town*** — how this particular book does it, with evidence from the text
3. **Spot it yourself** — a short task so you can practise finding it

You do not have to read these in order. If your teacher has set an essay, find the section you need.

**A warning about opinions.** Sections 11–14 (weak scenes, unnecessary scenes, repetition, confusing
sections) ask you to judge the book rather than praise it. That is a real exam skill and it is the one
students are worst at, because they think criticising a book means saying it is bad. It does not. It
means noticing how something is made and deciding whether it works. Every judgement in this guide gives
a reason. If you disagree with one, say so — but you must give a reason too, and quote the text.

**Read the book before you read this.** It will spoil the ending otherwise, and the ending is most of
the point.

## 1 · Plot

**What it means.** The plot is the sequence of events — what happens, in order. Do not confuse it with
the *story*, which is everything that happened including the parts the book does not show you. Plot is
what is on the page.

Most plots have a shape:

> **Exposition** (we meet the world) → **Inciting incident** (something goes wrong) → **Rising action**
> (complications pile up) → **Climax** (the turning point) → **Falling action** (the consequences) →
> **Resolution** (the new normal)

### The plot of *Mallam Town*

**Exposition — Chapter 1.** Kwame Asante, 44, a taxi driver at the Mallam Town rank, is called the
Peacemaker because he settles other people's quarrels by saying nothing. That same evening he and his
wife Mansa have the argument they have been having for months. He walks out to cool his head.

**Inciting incident — Chapters 1–2.** He comes back ten minutes later. Mansa is gone. Her perfume is
still in the air. There is only one door. She is not answering her phone — the call is *rejected*, twice.

**Rising action — Chapters 3–11.** Kwame drives out to look for her and is attacked on Dangerous Lane;
his taxi is stolen and he is left in the gutter with a dislocated shoulder. Nene, a 71-year-old
herbalist, finds him and treats him. He reports the theft at the police station. Five days pass and
nothing happens. Then the radio names **him** as the murderer of Efua Nanfuri, the daughter of the
Chief Inspector he reported to. He cannot go to the police. So he and Nene investigate themselves.

**Climax — Chapter 14.** At dawn, with a hundred people from the town watching, a carpenter opens a
secret door in the wall of an uncompleted house. Inside are the stripped remains of Kwame's taxi — and
Mansa, alive.

**Falling action — Chapter 15.** The landlord, Yaw Sarpong, tries to escape on an okada and is
permanently injured. Nene tends to him. Kwame is cleared.

**Resolution — Chapter 15, final page.** Kwame pushes the compound gate. It screams. *"This time,
everybody heard."*

### The engine of the plot

The plot runs on one piece of dramatic irony: **the reader is told the answer in Chapter 8, but Kwame
does not learn it until Chapter 14.**

Chapter 8 is Mansa's chapter. We learn exactly what happened to her: she could not sleep, she walked to
the uncompleted houses, she saw men unloading a truck, she recognised a face, she called the wrong
person about it, and she was taken. From Chapter 8 onwards, we know where she is. Kwame does not.

This changes what the suspense is. Before Chapter 8 we are asking *"what happened to Mansa?"* After
Chapter 8 we are asking *"when will he find her, and will he be in time?"* That is a deliberate
structural gamble — the book gives up its biggest mystery halfway through and replaces it with a
different question.

### Spot it yourself

Write out the plot as six sentences, one for each part of the shape above. Then try it again in three
sentences. Then in one. Being able to shrink a plot without losing it is the skill that gets marks.

## 2 · Characters

**What it means.** Characters are the people in the story. Two terms you need:

- **Round characters** change, have contradictions, and surprise you.
- **Flat characters** stay the same and exist to do one job.

Neither is better. A book full of round characters is exhausting.

### The main characters

| Character | Who they are | Round or flat? |
|---|---|---|
| **Kwame Asante** | 44, taxi driver, 20 years at the rank. Called the Peacemaker. | **Round** — the book's main change happens to him |
| **Nene** | 71, traditional herbalist. Bad knees, a stick, a basket. | **Round** — she changes most |
| **Mansa Asante** | 38, Kwame's wife. Deliberate in every motion. | **Round** — but we see her from the inside only once |
| **Inspector Adzo Mensah** | About 30. The officer who reads the file properly. | Round, but lightly drawn |
| **Kwabena Dapaah** | 54, carpenter. Built the door in the wall. | **Round** — Chapters 11 and 14 belong to him |
| **Chief Inspector Nanfuri** | Efua's father. Tired. | Round — he admits he was wrong |
| **Yaw Sarpong** | 56, landlord. Owns half of that side of Mallam Town. | **Flat** — the book deliberately keeps him unknowable |
| **Papa Kofi / Mr. Tee** | Drivers at the rank. Argue about everything. | Flat — comic relief and bookends |
| **The Old Woman on the veranda** | Watches everything, says nothing. | Flat, with a single late action |
| **Maame Adjoa / Maame Akua** | Mothers of two boys who vanished a year ago. | Flat, but they carry the book's anger |
| **Nartey** | Two doors from the Asantes. "He tells everybody he can sort things." | Flat — a function, not a person |
| **Efua Nanfuri** | 24. Dead before the book begins. Never appears. | Not a character at all — a *device* |

### Characters who are not people

Two "characters" are not human, and the book treats both as if they were:

- **The compound gate.** It has a personality — it *screams*, Kwame muffles it, Mansa oils it, and it
  gets the last word in the book.
- **Mallam Town itself.** The town has one consistent behaviour: it looks away. *"The Mallam Town
  reflex. See nothing. Survive."* The plot is largely about the town stopping doing that.

### Spot it yourself

Pick Sarpong and Nanfuri. Both are powerful men who do harm. Explain in a paragraph why the book lets
us inside Nanfuri's head (Chapter 14: *"I did it because the simplest story was the easiest one to grieve
with. That is not a reason."*) but never inside Sarpong's. What does the book gain by keeping Sarpong a
stranger?

## 3 · Character motivation

**What it means.** *Motivation* is the reason a character does what they do. Every action in a good book
comes from a want. If you cannot say what a character wants, you have not understood them.

### Kwame — wants peace, and is wrong about what peace is

For twenty years Kwame has believed that *"the fastest way to end a quarrel is to stop being in the room
with it."* That belief is why he walks out on Mansa. It is why he does not argue when men take his taxi:
*"the mathematics had never once favoured pride."* It is why he muffles the gate.

The whole book is this belief being tested and then broken. In Chapter 14 he says it out loud:

> *"I spent my whole life thinking peace meant keeping quiet. I do not believe that anymore."*

That is the book's thesis. Notice that he does not learn to shout. He learns to be heard.

### Nene — wants to stop being a wall

Nene's motivation is the most interesting in the book, and it takes the longest to appear. For forty
years she has been *"a woman with a shelf,"* listening to what people tell her and doing nothing with
it. Her anger surfaces in Chapter 7:

> *"A chief inspector's daughter dies and the whole town wakes up. Policemen on the road. A reward on the
> radio. Every station in Accra saying her name. Two boys from the rank disappear and the town goes back
> to sleep."*

That is the reason she helps Kwame. It is not kindness. It is a debt. And she says so in Chapter 10:

> *"They put you in my gutter. That is my work too."*

### Mansa — wants not to be ashamed

Mansa's night is driven entirely by small, human, embarrassing wants. She puts on perfume *"because she
was going out, and because she was going to meet a man, and because she was thirty-eight years old and
she was not going to meet a man at midnight looking as though she had been sitting up all night."* She
oils the gate *"because she did not want the whole compound to know she was going out at midnight to
meet a man about a thing she had not told her husband."*

**This is the most important motivation in the book, and the easiest to misread.** Mansa is not reckless
and she is not unfaithful. She is ashamed of going behind her husband's back, so she is careful about
noise. *"Shame makes you careful about noise. That was all it was."* The book insists on this because
the town will later read the perfume and the oiled gate as evidence that she ran away willingly.

### Kwabena Dapaah — wants to stop carrying it

> *"I did not ask. That is the thing I have been living with. I did not ask, because a man pays you and
> you do not ask him questions. And I had three children in school that year."*

Kwabena is not brave. He comes back because he cannot sleep, and he confesses because the shame of
building the door is worse than the risk of admitting it. That is a more believable motive than courage,
and the book is right to choose it.

### Inspector Adzo Mensah — wants the file to make sense

> *"I have been reading the file on Efua Nanfuri for three weeks. Every page. Nobody asked me to. That is
> not a virtue, it is just how I am, and it is why I am here."*

Mensah is the only character motivated purely by accuracy. Note that she is also the only character who
says out loud what everyone else feels but cannot prove.

### Nanfuri — wants grief to be simple

Nanfuri is not a villain. He is a man with *"a daughter in the ground"* who reaches for the simplest
story because the complicated one hurts more. His admission in Chapter 14 is the most honest line any
adult says in the book.

### Spot it yourself

Choose any character and finish this sentence with evidence from the text:
*"______ wants ______, and the proof is on page ______ where they ______."*
If you cannot finish it, you have found a character the book has not fully built.

## 4 · Pacing

**What it means.** Pacing is the speed at which the story moves. A writer controls it with three dials:

- **Scene length** — short chapters feel fast
- **Summary vs. scene** — "five days passed" is fast; a conversation is slow
- **Sentence length** — short sentences speed a reader up

### The pace of *Mallam Town*

| Chapters | Pace | How it is done |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | **Fast** | One night. Short chapters. The inciting incident lands inside 1,600 words. |
| 3 | **Fastest** | 583 words, the shortest chapter in the book. The attack is told in short, flat sentences. |
| 4–5 | **Slow** | 1,390 + 841 words. Recovery and bureaucracy. Long scenes, low stakes. |
| 6 | **Fast turn** | The radio. The book pivots in a single page. |
| 7–8 | **Slow** | Exposition and then Mansa alone in a room watching a square of light. |
| 9–10 | **Medium** | Investigation. Dialogue drives it. |
| 11 | **Stops dead** | *"Three weeks went by."* |
| 12–13 | **Fast** | The raid, the chase, the alley, the knock at the door. |
| 14 | **Slow, then huge** | Dawn, a hundred people, one chisel, one door. |
| 15 | **Medium, then still** | The crash, then the walk home, then the gate. |

### The one thing worth arguing about

**Chapter 11 stops the book for three weeks.** Almost every thriller accelerates after its midpoint. This
one decelerates, hard. Look at what is in that gap:

> *"It went the way time goes when you are waiting for somebody else to move first — a child's fever, a
> woman with a bad back, a quarrel two compounds over that Nene settled without leaving her chair."*

And Nene's line to a man who cannot sit still:

> *"Things come. That is the whole skill — knowing when to sit."*

This is a real gamble. Three weeks of nothing in a 15,000-word book is a fifth of the book spent waiting.
Whether it works depends on whether you find waiting *interesting* or *boring* — and that is a genuine
matter of taste, not something this guide can settle for you.

**What it is for, if it works:** the book's subject is a town that will not look, and a woman who has
spent forty years not acting. A plot that simply raced to the discovery would prove nothing. The waiting
*is* the argument.

### Spot it yourself

Cut Chapter 11's three-week gap to three days and rewrite the paragraph above. Does anything in Chapters
12–15 stop making sense? (Hint: check Inspector Mensah's *"three weeks"* in Chapter 13, and Kwame's
*"three weeks and more ago"* about the mirror.) Now put the three weeks back. What does the book gain?

## 5 · Dialogue

**What it means.** Dialogue is what characters say. Good dialogue does three jobs at once: it sounds
like a real person, it moves the plot, and it reveals character. If a line only does one of those, cut it.

### How *Mallam Town* writes speech

**1 · Very short lines.** Nene in particular:

> *"Sit."* — *"I know."* — *"Yes."* — *"Thursday."* — *"No."*

**2 · Characters who do not answer the question asked.** In Chapter 1:

> *"My credit finished this morning," she said, not looking up. "Yours?"*
> He slid it across the table without a word.
> *"The balance."*
> He heard it and did not answer.
> *"Kwame. The MoMo balance."*

Nobody says what they mean. The reader has to work out that this is a marriage arguing about money
without ever using the word.

**3 · People who say the opposite of what they feel.** Mansa's two rejected calls are not dialogue at
all — that is the point. The most important conversation in the first two chapters is one that does not
happen.

**4 · The newsreader.** All broadcast speech is in *italics*, which sets it apart as a kind of official
voice — flat, impersonal, and wrong:

> *"Asante is known locally as 'The Peacemaker.' Members of the public are warned not to approach him."*

Notice what the book does: it takes Kwame's nickname — earned in Chapter 1 as a compliment — and has the
radio use it as a weapon. That is the cruelest moment in the book and it contains no violence at all.

**5 · Speech that is really about something else.** Kwabena's confession:

> *"I came for my hands," he said.*
> A beat. Both men knew that was not why he had come.

### The most important line of dialogue in the book

> *"Everything in this town happens in the dark," she said. "Let us have this one in the light, for once."*

That is Nene, at the end of Chapter 13. It is the plan, the theme, and the title of the next chapter all
at once.

### Spot it yourself

Find three lines of dialogue where a character means something other than what the words say. For each,
write what they literally said, and what they actually meant.

## 6 · Suspense

**What it means.** Suspense is the feeling of wanting to know what happens next. Writers build it with
three main tools:

- **Dramatic irony** — the reader knows something a character does not
- **Foreshadowing** — a hint of what is coming
- **The cliffhanger** — ending a chapter at a moment of unresolved tension

### The two suspenses

***Mallam Town* runs two different suspense machines, one after the other.** This is the cleverest thing
about its construction.

**Chapters 1–7: we know what Kwame knows.** We are asking *"what happened to Mansa?"* The suspense comes
from not knowing. Key techniques:

- The phone is *"warm"* — somebody has just been holding it
- *"He could smell her and not find her. And there was only one door."*
- The call log has a number he does not recognise — *"He did not look at it properly."*
- *"Not even the gate"* — the Old Woman heard nothing, which is impossible

**Chapters 8–14: we know more than Kwame.** Chapter 8 answers the question completely. From that point
the suspense is dramatic irony — we know Mansa is in the third house; Kwame finds the wrapper in Chapter
12 and still does not know she is one room away.

### Foreshadowing to trace

- Chapter 1: *"He did not yet know how much that belief was going to cost him."*
- Chapter 1: *"Tonight it did not scream."* → explained in Chapter 8, corrected in Chapter 14
- Chapter 3: *"Leave his hands,"* and *"Not the face"* → Nene works out why in Chapter 4
- Chapter 4: Nene says she would send him to hospital *"if his head were bleeding inside"* → the clinic
  record saves his life in Chapter 13
- Chapter 5: Nanfuri is *"carrying something… it was in the set of the shoulders"* → we learn what in
  Chapter 6

### Chapter endings

Almost every chapter ends on a turn or an unanswered question. Check these:

- Ch. 3 ends with a phone ringing that nobody hears
- Ch. 5 ends: *"Nene was right, he thought. What he did not think, because it did not occur to him, was
  that Nene was usually right."*
- Ch. 7 ends: *"Somebody used your phone that night. Somebody who did not want the call on their own."*
- Ch. 12 ends with three taps on the door and one word: *"Police."*
- Ch. 13 ends: *"Dawn."*

### Spot it yourself

Chapter 12 ends with the word *"Police."* Chapter 13 opens with the knock. Does the reader believe, for
even one sentence, that Kwame is about to be arrested? Find the exact sentence where that fear dies.

## 7 · Setting

**What it means.** Setting is where and when the story happens. It is never just a backdrop — in a good
book the setting is part of the argument.

### Mallam Town

The book is set in a real place: Mallam, in the Greater Accra Region of Ghana. The whole story lasts
about five weeks. Almost nothing in it could happen anywhere else.

**The five settings and what each one means:**

| Place | What happens there | What it stands for |
|---|---|---|
| **The taxi rank** | Kwame works, argues are settled, news arrives | Ordinary life; the town's public face |
| **The compound house** | Kwame and Mansa's room; the gate; the Old Woman's veranda | Private life; what neighbours see and don't |
| **Dangerous Lane** | The attack | The town's known danger, made ordinary by habit |
| **The uncompleted houses** | Where Mansa is held; where the door is | The town's blind spot — in plain sight for four years |
| **Nene's room** | Treatment, confession, planning | The one place where people tell the truth |

### Setting as argument

Notice that the buildings where the worst things happen are **unfinished**. They have been going up for
four years. Everybody knows about them. *"Everybody said they were bad luck."* Nobody looks.

That is the book's whole point, stated in bricks. The horror is not hidden. It is simply not looked at.

### Detail as evidence

The setting is built out of specific, ordinary Ghanaian detail — and that detail is doing real work:

- **The side mirror** — polished with *"a rag far cleaner than the car deserved"*, later found cracked in
  a heap of stripped parts. One object, two chapters, thirty pages apart.
- **Kenkey, trotro, MoMo, okada, the MTN kiosk** — the book does not explain these, because the reader
  it is written for does not need them explained. That is a decision, and a respectful one.
- **The gate** — *"Rusted, crooked at the top, the post bent beyond repair."* It has been broken the
  whole time; it is only at the end that anyone makes a noise with it.

### Time of day

Watch how often the book mentions light. Almost every important thing happens at night: the argument
(11:42 p.m.), the disappearance, the attack (00:30), the raid, the knock at the door. The climax is
deliberately scheduled:

> *"Everything in this town happens in the dark. Let us have this one in the light, for once."*

Chapter 14 is called **Dawn at the Uncompleted House**. That is not a poetic title. It is the plan.

### Spot it yourself

The square of light on Mansa's wall is the only clock she has: *"That was how she knew what day it was."*
List every other object in the book that is used to measure time or light. What do they have in common?

## 8 · Point of view

**What it means.** Point of view (POV) is *who is telling the story* and *how much they know*.

- **First person** — "I" — a character tells it
- **Third person limited** — "he/she" — the narrator stays inside one character's head
- **Third person omniscient** — the narrator knows everything, including what everyone is thinking

### The main point of view

***Mallam Town* is told in the **third person**, and for most of the book it stays inside Kwame's head.**
We know what he knows. When he does not know where Mansa is, neither do we.

### The three chapters that leave him

| Chapter | Whose head we are in | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Chapter 4** (opening) | **Nene's** | We watch her treat an unconscious man and draw her own conclusions — *"These people were not animals. They were workers, doing a job."* Kwame cannot know this. |
| **Chapter 8** | **Mansa's** — entirely | This is the pivot of the whole book. The reader is given the answer Kwame is still looking for. |
| **Chapter 11** (middle) | **Kwabena Dapaah's** | Three nights of a man failing to sleep. We know he is coming back before anyone in Nene's compound does. |

Moving to Nene's head lets the book show us her skill. Moving to Mansa's head gives us the answer. Moving
to Kwabena's head lets us watch a conscience work. Three different jobs, three different chapters.

### The narrator's one unusual habit

The narrator occasionally steps outside the story's time and speaks from the future:

- *"That was the thing he would remember afterwards"* (Ch. 3)
- *"one of the things Kwame would remember about her afterwards"* (Ch. 9)
- *"the thing Kwame remembered afterwards, and the thing he went on remembering for years"* (Ch. 15)

This is a small, consistent trick. It tells you two things: that the narrator knows how it ends, and
that the ending is survivable. It is a quiet promise to a young reader that the character gets through.

### The one place the book goes omniscient

Chapter 15, after the crash:

> *"They said afterwards that he would not walk again. Kwame never heard anybody say they were sorry
> about it. He never heard anybody say they were glad."*

The narrator suddenly knows what the whole town said and did not say, over years. That widening is
deliberate — it is the only moment the book speaks for Mallam Town as a whole.

### Spot it yourself

Rewrite the opening of Chapter 3 (the attack on Dangerous Lane) in Mansa's first person. What does the
reader lose? Now try it in Nene's. What does the reader gain?

## 9 · Chapter structure

**What it means.** How the book is divided, why the divisions fall where they do, and what each chapter
is built to achieve.

### The shape of the book

Fifteen chapters, roughly 15,500 words, averaging about 1,000 words each. But the chapters are very
uneven, and the unevenness is deliberate:

| Chapter | Words | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 3 · Dangerous Lane | 583 | Shortest. The attack. Speed. |
| 8 · Mansa | 1,515 | Longest with Ch. 14. The answer. Interiority. |
| 14 · Dawn at the Uncompleted House | 1,539 | The climax. Needs room. |
| 4 · Nene | 1,390 | Second-longest. Recovery is slow because bodies are slow. |

**Short chapters for violence, long chapters for revelation.** The attack that changes everything gets
583 words. The moment a carpenter fits a chisel into a wall gets 1,539.

### Titles

Every chapter is titled after a single thing — a person, a place, an object:

> The Peacemaker · The Empty Room · Dangerous Lane · Nene · The Report · A Face on the Radio · What Nene
> Knows · Mansa · What Mansa Saw · The Plan · The Carpenter · The Narrow Escape · The Honest Inspector ·
> Dawn at the Uncompleted House · The Okada

Notice the pattern in the middle of the book. **Chapters 4 and 8 are named after the two women, and
chapters 7 and 9 are named after what they know and saw.** The men's chapters are named after objects
and events. That is not an accident: the book's argument is that the women know things and the men do
things.

### How chapters end

Almost every chapter ends on a hook. See §6 above. The exceptions are worth noticing: Chapters 4 and 15
end quietly, on rest and on a gate. The book opens on noise and closes on noise — but the first noise is
suppressed and the last one is not.

### The three-act structure

- **Act 1 (Chapters 1–5):** the disappearance and the attack. Ends with the perfume fading.
- **Act 2 (Chapters 6–11):** the investigation. Ends with a confession — *"I built the door."*
- **Act 3 (Chapters 12–15):** the discovery and the reckoning.

### Spot it yourself

Chapter 11 contains *two* endings: Kwabena walks out without his medicine, and then four nights later he
comes back and confesses. Why not put the confession in Chapter 12? What would be lost?

## 10 · Continuity

**What it means.** Continuity is whether the details hold together — whether things said early match
things said later. It is the part of a book a writer can most easily get wrong, and the part a careful
reader can most easily catch.

### The chains — do these hold?

**The phone.** Ch. 2: Kwame pockets it and drives out. Ch. 3: the robbers take it and drop it in the
dust; it rings twice, unanswered. Ch. 4: Nene picks it up and says nothing. **Ch. 7: she produces it
from her drawer** — *"I picked it up where I picked you up"* — and Kwame finally reads the call log.
Ch. 10: it goes to market in Nene's basket to Ama at the MTN kiosk. Ch. 13: Inspector Mensah traces it
officially. **Holds.** Note the two channels are deliberately different: Nene gets a name, the police get
the connection.

**The side mirror.** Ch. 1: polished with a rag *"far cleaner than the car deserved."* Ch. 12: found
cracked in the heap, *"he had put his thumb over that chip a thousand times."* Ch. 14: identifies the
taxi. **Holds.**

**The gate.** Ch. 1: it does not scream. Ch. 2: *"Not even the gate."* Ch. 8: Mansa oiled it.
Ch. 14: *"I opened it myself. That is why it did not scream. He did not leave me. I went."* **Holds, and
pays off.**

**The perfume.** Strong in Ch. 1–2 → fading in Ch. 5 → reframed in Ch. 8 (*"It did not mean anything"*)
→ **gone from the wrapper in Ch. 12** (*"there was nothing"*). **Holds.** This is the book's most careful
thread: the smell fading is how we track how long she has been gone.

**The hands.** Ch. 3: *"Leave his hands."* Ch. 4: Nene turns them over *"the way you check a fish"* and
finds them unmarked. Ch. 11: Kwabena comes to Nene about *his* hands. **Holds.**

**The clinic record.** Ch. 4: Nene says she would send him to hospital if his head were bleeding inside —
but he never goes. Ch. 13 resolves it: she sent for a nurse and had it documented. **Holds, and this is
the thread that saves his life.**

**KWESI.** Ch. 7: Nene names two boys, Kwesi (17) and Ekow (19). Ch. 14: a toolbag with KWESI painted on
the side. *"I have been angry about that for a long time. Now it has a name on it."* **Holds, and it is
the emotional climax of the book.**

### Two continuity problems — see if you agree

**Problem 1 · Two "Monday night"s.** This is the real one.

In Chapter 5, Kwame tells the desk officer his taxi was taken *"about half past twelve Monday night."*
In Chapter 6, the radio says the vehicle was used in an armed robbery *"on Monday night"* — the robbery
in which Efua Nanfuri died. In Chapter 13, Inspector Mensah builds her whole case on these being
**different Mondays, a week apart**:

> *"You reported that taxi stolen four days before the robbery it was used in. A man who means to use his
> own car in a robbery does not put his own name against it in a police ledger four days early."*

The logic is sound. The presentation is not. **Nothing on the page tells the reader these are two
different Mondays**, and "Monday night" appears for both events. A reader who assumes it is the same
Monday will think Kwame reported the theft *after* the murder — at which point the single most important
plot point in the book collapses.

The signals that they are different weeks are there but quiet: *"Five days passed"* at the end of
Chapter 5, and Mansa's *"eighth day"* in Chapter 8. That is not enough for a piece of information this
load-bearing.

*If you were editing this: Chapter 6 or Chapter 13 needs one plain sentence — "the following Monday" or
"Monday, a week after they took my car." The book currently asks the reader to do arithmetic at the exact
moment it should be clearest.*

**Problem 2 · The title of Chapter 15.** The last chapter is called **The Okada** in the manuscript and
in the contents page, but the illustration brief calls it **The Gate**. The illustration for the chapter,
and the cover of the book, are both of the gate. One of these is wrong, and it should be settled before
printing.

### Spot it yourself

Trace one object of your own through the book from first appearance to last. Say where it appears, and
what it means at each appearance. Good candidates: the wrapper, the stick, the basket, the radio, the
tyre iron, the yam.

## 11 · Weak scenes

**What it means.** A weak scene is one that does less than the scenes around it. Common causes: the
stakes are unclear, the outcome is known in advance, the dialogue explains what we already know, or the
scene is there because the plot needs it rather than because the characters do.

Judging a scene weak is not the same as disliking it. Give a reason.

### Candidate 1 · Chapter 12, the escape (the book's most convenient moment)

Kwame is cornered in an alley — one man in front, one behind, no way out — and Nene happens to be there
with the entire compound.

The book tries to earn this. Nene said in Chapter 10 that she would gather people, and Chapter 11 gives
her three weeks to do it. So it is *motivated*. But it is still a rescue arriving at the exact second of
maximum danger, and that is the oldest trick in the book.

**The defence:** the point of the scene is not the rescue. It is *"I am looking for my daughter"* — a
question nobody can refuse to witness — and the fact that the crowd shows its **faces**. That is the
town's whole arc in one image, and it is worth the coincidence.

**The case against:** Kwame, the book's active character, is reduced to sitting down on the ground while
an old woman saves him. He does not act again until Chapter 14.

### Candidate 2 · Chapter 15, Sarpong's arrival

Sarpong arrives on an okada at the exact moment a hundred people are standing at his secret door. It is
possible — someone would have called him. But the book never says who called him, or why he came himself
rather than sending someone, and his arrival exists almost entirely so that the ending can happen.

Notice how fast it is: he arrives, sees, runs. Three beats. The book is in a hurry to be finished with
him.

### Candidate 3 · Chapter 5, "Five days passed"

> *"Five days in which Kwame went to the rank and asked, and the drivers shook their heads…"*
> *"Five days in which Nene asked after Mansa the way old women do…"*
> *"Five days in which nobody from the Mallam Town Police Post came to the compound."*

Structurally necessary — the book needs dead time for the radio twist to land. But it is the closest the
book comes to telling us things instead of showing us, and the three-sentence pattern is more like a
list than a scene.

### Candidate 4 · Chapter 6, the radio

The newsreader happens to say exactly what the plot needs, twice, at the right moment. Nene even slaps
the radio to make it work. Exposition delivered by appliance.

**The defence:** the *second* broadcast is not exposition, it is the cruelty — the radio using Kwame's
nickname against him. The first one is the weaker of the two.

### Spot it yourself

Choose the scene you think is weakest and defend it. Then argue the opposite. Which argument did you find
easier to make with evidence?

## 12 · Unnecessary scenes

**What it means.** A scene is *weak* if it does its job badly; it is *unnecessary* if the book would work
the same without it. The test: **cut it and see what breaks.**

### The yam scene — which appears twice

Chapter 11:

> *"She was paid in yam. The mother said it was too much yam and Nene said it was not nearly enough, and
> they went back and forth about it for ten minutes while Kwame sat in the corner vibrating."*

Chapter 15:

> *"A woman came up from the market with three yams in a cloth and pressed them on her, and Nene said it
> was too much, and the woman said it was not nearly enough, and they went back and forth about it for a
> while."*

**Cut one?** No — but it is worth knowing why both are there. The first shows Kwame's frustration with
Nene's refusal to hurry, immediately before she tells him *"knowing when to sit."* The second shows the
town's relationship with her after everything: she is still just an old woman being paid in yam. The joke
repeats so that we can see what has and has not changed.

**But:** the second one is very close to a repeat of the first. If either had to go, the second could be
shortened to one line without loss.

### What is genuinely necessary

- **The Old Woman on the veranda** (Ch. 1) — two lines, and she is the one who establishes that *"not
  even the gate"* made no sound. Indispensable.
- **Kwabena planing wood for four nights** (Ch. 11) — no plot at all. Four paragraphs of a man ruining a
  piece of wood because he cannot sleep. This is where his confession becomes believable. Keep.
- **The two bottles** (Ch. 7) — Kwesi and Ekow, named on scratched glass. This is what gives Chapter 14's
  toolbag its power. Keep.

### What might be cut

- The second yam exchange could lose half its length.
- Chapter 4's treatment sequence is long. But it establishes Nene's competence, her limits, and the
  clinic record — three things the ending depends on. Cut nothing.

### Spot it yourself

Take Chapter 5's "five days passed" section. Rewrite it as a single scene — one thing that happens on one
of those days. Does it do the same job better, or does the summary work because it is a summary?

## 13 · Repetition

**What it means.** Repetition is one of a writer's strongest tools. Used deliberately it becomes a
**motif** — a repeating element that gathers meaning each time it returns. Used carelessly it is just
the writer saying the same thing twice.

### The motifs — where each one appears

**The gate's scream.**
> Ch. 1: *"Tonight it did not scream."* → Ch. 2: *"Not even the gate."* → Ch. 8: Mansa oiled it →
> Ch. 14: *"I opened it myself."* → Ch. 15: it screams, and *"this time, everybody heard."*

Five appearances, and the last one inverts the first. This is the book's central motif and it is
flawlessly handled.

**Nobody looks at an old woman.**
> Ch. 7: *"Nobody watches an old woman, Kwame. That is the whole use of me."* →
> Ch. 10: *"Nobody stops an old woman."* → Ch. 12: *"who tells an old woman anything?"*

Three times, in three different voices. Each time she turns the town's disrespect into a tool.

**Hands.**
> Ch. 3: *"Leave his hands."* → Ch. 4: unmarked, *"the way you check a fish"* → Ch. 4: *"a taxi driver
> needs his hands"* → Ch. 11: Kwabena's hands → Ch. 14: Kwame holds his hands out, *"palms turned up"* →
> Ch. 14: *"I am here to show you whose hands did it."*

This is the most worked motif in the book. The robbers spare his hands so he can work; at the end he
offers his open hands as evidence, and the phrase "whose hands did it" completes it.

**The rag and the mirror.**
> *"a rag far cleaner than the car deserved"* — Ch. 1, and again in Ch. 12, and again in Ch. 13.

**Going still.**
> Ch. 6: Nene stops working with her hands, *"which for Nene was the same as shouting."* →
> Ch. 13: Inspector Mensah *"went very still — the way Nene had gone still in the alley."*

The second one tells you the two women are the same kind of person without ever saying so.

**Dark and light.**
> Epigraph: *"Everything in this town happens in the dark."* → Ch. 13, repeated → Ch. 14's title: *Dawn.*
> → Ch. 14: *"They brought Mansa out into the daylight."* → Ch. 14: Mansa *"told the truth in her own
> voice, in front of everybody."*

### Repetition that may be accidental

- **"I know."** Nene says it in Chapters 6, 9, and 12. Effective each time, but three is a lot.
- **"That is the whole of it" / "That is the whole use of me" / "That is the whole skill."** Nene has a
  verbal tic for summing things up. It appears in Chapters 4, 7, 10, and 11. It is characteristic, but
  four is probably one too many.
- **The radio plays "an advertisement for a funeral home."** Chapters 6 and 9. A small, grim joke, twice.

### Spot it yourself

Pick the **hands** motif. Write down every appearance with its chapter number. Then answer: at which
appearance does it stop being about hands and start being about blame?

## 14 · Confusing sections

**What it means.** Anything a careful reader can reasonably misread. Some confusion is deliberate — the
book is not obliged to explain itself on the first page. Some is not.

### 1 · The two Monday nights (the serious one)

Covered in §10. **The most important fact in the book is stated in a way that invites the reader to get
it wrong.** If you are confused about it, the book is at fault, not you.

Test yourself: *How many days before Efua Nanfuri died did Kwame report his taxi stolen?* If you had to
re-read to answer that, you have found the problem.

### 2 · Nartey and Nanfuri

Two names, both starting with **N**, both connected to the police, one a good man and one a bad one:

- **Chief Inspector Nanfuri** — the senior officer, Efua's father, the man who puts Kwame on the radio
- **Nartey** — the man two doors away, paid by Sarpong, the person Mansa wrongly trusted

They never appear in the same scene. For a reader aged twelve meeting these names three chapters apart,
this is a genuine trap. If you mixed them up, that is a design problem in the book, not a failure of
attention.

### 3 · Who Efua Nanfuri is

Efua never appears and is dead before the book starts. She is named on the radio in Chapter 6 and
referred to about six times after that. If you lost track of her, you are not alone — she is a plot
device (the reason anyone investigates) rather than a character.

### 4 · The three earlier victims

Chapter 7 introduces: **Kwesi** (17) and **Ekow** (19), apprentices who vanished *"a year ago last
March"*, and **a construction worker** who vanished six months ago. Three missing people, two of them
named, none of them on the page. Only Kwesi pays off — in Chapter 14, with the toolbag. Ekow and the
construction worker do not.

It is worth asking whether the book should have paid off all three, or only one. As written, Kwesi's bag
is devastating and the other two are arithmetic.

### 5 · The four-day gap

*"You reported your taxi stolen four days before the robbery it was used in."* Thursday to Monday is four
days counting inclusively, three days exclusively. Either is defensible, but the book should pick one and
be consistent — a reader who counts carefully gets a different number from a reader who does not.

### 6 · Deliberate, and not a problem

- **Why the robbers spared his hands and face** — explained in Chapter 4 by Nene. Beautiful deduction.
- **Why the gate did not scream** — planted in Chapter 1, answered in Chapter 8. This is exactly how
  mystery should work: the answer is hidden in plain sight and makes you want to re-read.
- **Chief Inspector Nanfuri's phone call in Chapter 5** — *"give me till Friday"* — is unreadable on the
  first pass and clear on the second. That is intentional and it works.

### Spot it yourself

Make a list of every question you had while reading, and mark each one **answered later**, **answered
immediately**, or **never answered**. Which column is longest? What does that tell you about the kind of
book this is?

## 15 · Ending

**What it means.** An ending must do two things: **resolve the plot** and **answer the theme**. A book can
do the first and fail the second, which is why some endings feel hollow.

### What happens

Sarpong arrives, sees the open door, runs, falls from an okada, and is run over — **described entirely by
sound**:

> *"The sound was not a scream. It was a noise Kwame had heard once, years ago, at the rank, and had never
> managed to forget: the ordinary, muffled sound of a body in the way of something heavy."*

Nene tends to him: *"It is not finished for him. It is beginning."* Then the town walks home, Nene
complains about her knees, a woman gives her yam, and Kwame pushes the gate.

### The three things the ending refuses to do

**1 · It refuses a mob.** This is the bravest thing in the book.

> *"that not one person in a hundred put out a hand. No shout. No stone. Nothing at all. They simply
> parted, the way a crowd parts, and let him through."*

A hundred people who have every reason to kill Sarpong do nothing. The book's argument is that the town
must learn to *look*, not to *punish* — and looking is harder.

**2 · It refuses a happy reunion.** Kwame and Mansa do not embrace.

> *"They looked at each other across four feet of concrete floor with a hundred people watching, and
> neither of them moved, because there was too much to move through first."*

And the book's last word on them: *"There would be time for that. There would be a long time for it, and
it would not be easy, and it was beginning — which was the thing that mattered."*

For a book written for twelve-to-fifteen-year-olds, refusing to pretend that a marriage is fixed by a
rescue is a genuinely mature choice. It is also the truthful one.

**3 · It refuses to resolve Sarpong's fate into justice or pity.** *"Kwame never heard anybody say they
were sorry about it. He never heard anybody say they were glad."* The book declines to tell you how to
feel, which is unusual and correct.

### The last image

> *"Then he pushed, and it screamed — a long metal shriek that went the whole length of the rank and out
> into the road, loud and rusty and impossible to ignore."*
> *"This time, everybody heard."*

In Chapter 1 the same gate opened in silence, and Kwame muffled it *"the way he had learned to do
everything in his life: quietly, and at his own expense."* The last sentence of the book is the exact
inversion of the man we met on page one.

**He has not learned to shout. He has stopped swallowing the noise.**

### How the theme is answered

| The question the book asks | Where it is answered |
|---|---|
| What does it mean to be a peacemaker? | Ch. 14: *"I spent my whole life thinking peace meant keeping quiet. I do not believe that anymore."* |
| Does a town have to look away? | Ch. 14: a hundred people walk out at dawn and watch |
| Is Nene's anger justified? | Ch. 14: *"Now it has a name on it."* |
| Does the truth need the police? | Ch. 13: *"You have the paper. I have the people. Neither of us had enough alone."* |

### Is it a happy ending?

Partly. Mansa is alive. Kwame is cleared. The boys have a name on a bag. But two boys are still dead, a
construction worker is still missing, a young woman is still dead, and a marriage has *"a long time"* of
difficulty ahead of it.

The book ends where it began — at the rank, with horns and a kenkey seller — *"as though nothing at all
had happened."* That line is doing a lot of work. Everything happened. The town is the same. But the gate
screams now.

### Spot it yourself

Write the last paragraph of the book yourself, in under 60 words, without using the gate. Now compare it
to the one that is there. What does the gate do that your ending does not?

## Essay and exam practice

Five questions that would each earn marks in a JHS literature exam. Answer any of them in 300–400 words
with at least three quotations.

1. **"The book is not really about a missing woman. It is about a town that will not look."** How far do
   you agree?
2. Write about the character who changes most in *Mallam Town*. What changes them?
3. How does the writer make Chapter 14 (Dawn at the Uncompleted House) the most important chapter in the
   book?
4. *Mallam Town* is set in a real place. How does the writer make the setting important to the story?
5. "A book for young readers should never end unhappily." Using *Mallam Town*, argue for or against.

### A note on answering

The strongest answers do three things:

- **Quote short.** Four or five words are better than a whole sentence.
- **Say what the quote does**, not just what it says. Not *"the writer says the gate screamed"* but
  *"the writer makes the gate scream to show that Kwame has stopped hiding."*
- **Answer the actual question.** Question 1 says *"how far do you agree?"* — that means you must say how
  much, and why not more.

## Quick reference — the fifteen terms

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| **Plot** | What happens, in the order the writer chose to tell it |
| **Characters** | The people; *round* ones change, *flat* ones do a job |
| **Motivation** | What a character wants, and why they want it |
| **Pacing** | How fast the story moves, and how the writer controls it |
| **Dialogue** | What characters say — and what they mean by it |
| **Suspense** | Wanting to know what happens next |
| **Setting** | Where and when — and what the place *means* |
| **Point of view** | Who is telling the story and how much they know |
| **Chapter structure** | How the book is divided and why |
| **Continuity** | Whether the details hold together |
| **Weak scenes** | Scenes that do their job badly — with a reason |
| **Unnecessary scenes** | Scenes the book would survive without |
| **Repetition** | A repeated element that gathers meaning (a *motif*) |
| **Confusing sections** | What a careful reader can reasonably misread |
| **Ending** | Resolving the plot *and* answering the theme |
