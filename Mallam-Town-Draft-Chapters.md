# Chapter 1 — The Peacemaker



Mallam Town woke up at four and did not wait for anybody.

By seven the taxi rank was already full of dust and shouting and horns. Old yellow taxis nosed in one after another, their paint faded by the sun. Drivers leaned against bonnets arguing about fuel prices. A kenkey seller went past calling out her prices. At the corner a preacher's public address system coughed into life, far too early, and a hundred people pretended not to hear it.

Kwame Asante sat on an overturned plastic crate beside his own taxi, polishing his side mirror with a rag. The rag was far cleaner than the car deserved.

When the preacher got going, Kwame listened for a while, and then opened the bible on his knee to check the verse. He did this most mornings. Not to argue — to see whether the man was telling the truth. If he found that he was not, Kwame simply stopped listening. He had never once said anything to anybody about it.

He was forty-four, tall and lanky, with quiet hands and a face that did not hurry to show what he was thinking. Twenty years at this rank had taught him that most arguments are not about what they say they are about, and that nearly all of them go quiet on their own if nobody feeds them.

Peace, in Kwame's life, meant keeping quiet long enough for other people's noise to die down.

He did not yet know how much that belief was going to cost him.

---

At the other end of the rank, Papa Kofi and Mr. Tee were arguing again.

"You are telling me the man paid you for three?" Mr. Tee said.

"He paid me for two and walked away on the third," Papa Kofi said. "So who is the thief here?"

They had been at it since six. The argument had started somewhere else entirely and had only now found its way to the station.

Kwame did not look up from his mirror.

"It was not my customer," Mr. Tee said.

"It was yours."

"It was not."

Kwame waited until they had both run out of words. Then he said, without raising his voice:

"Was the man wearing a blue shirt?"

Mr. Tee turned. "Yes —"

"Then that is the one Papa Kofi dropped at the junction an hour ago. Not yours."

A beat.

Mr. Tee opened his mouth. Closed it again. He muttered something about the price of fuel and walked off to his car.

Papa Kofi grinned and clapped Kwame on the shoulder.

"Peacemaker," he said. "Every time."

Kwame shrugged and went back to the mirror.

It was true, what they called him. At the rank, in the compound where he lived, even at the roadside food stalls — Kwame was the man people brought their small quarrels to. He listened. He did not shout. He settled things. He had been doing it so long that he hardly noticed anymore.

---

That evening, at the compound house off the main road, his wife Mansa sat on their small porch braiding her hair.

Mansa Asante was thirty-eight and deliberate in every motion. Even braiding her hair she did it slowly, carefully, as if the world would wait for her to finish. A small mirror was propped against the wall. A pot simmered on a single gas stove beside her. The smell of stew mixed with something else — her perfume, sweet and strong, filling the small room before you saw her face properly.

She had changed her cloth while he was washing. He noticed it and did not think about it.

"My credit finished this morning," she said, not looking up. "Yours?"

He slid it across the table without a word. She used it for a moment, then set it down.

Then: "The balance."

He heard it and did not answer.

"Kwame. The MoMo balance."

It was the second time this month she had asked in that particular voice — the one that was not really a question.

"Ei," he said, and went on washing.

She said nothing more. She went back to her hair. The pot went on simmering. The two of them sat in the same small room not saying the thing they were both thinking, which was what they had been doing for months.

---

Later, the wall clock read 11:42 p.m.

He could not have said afterwards which of them had said the thing that finished it. It was the same argument they had been having for months, arriving at the same place it always arrived. And when it was over Kwame stood up and walked out — not to punish her, not to end anything, but because he did not want to fuel it. Twenty years at the rank had taught him that the fastest way to end a quarrel is to stop being in the room with it.

Outside, in the compound yard, the Old Woman on the veranda watched him pass. She was somewhere in her seventies, thin as a broomstick, and she sat in that same chair every evening, watching everything and saying almost nothing. Her chair creaked once as Kwame went by. She did not speak.

Kwame reached the rusted compound gate.

Every night when he came home late, this gate screamed — a long metal shriek that woke half the compound. So Kwame had learned to push it slowly, gently, muffling the sound with his own hand. The way he had learned to do everything in his life: quietly, and at his own expense.

Tonight it did not scream.

He pushed it the way he always did and it opened in silence. For half a second some part of him noticed. Then he was thinking about the argument again, and the half-second passed, and he walked out into the road.

Fifty metres down, he stopped.

"My phone," he said to the empty road.

And he turned back.

# Chapter 2 — The Empty Room

He walked back the way he had come, and by the time he reached the compound the anger had already drained out of his shoulders, the way it always did once he put distance between himself and a fight.

That was why he walked out. Not to punish Mansa. To cool his own head.

The yard was dark. The Old Woman's chair was empty. Somewhere a radio played too low to hear the words.

He pushed open the door to their room.

Darkness.

Wrong darkness. He had left the light on. He knew he had left the light on, the way you know a thing you did with your own hand thirty seconds ago.

"Mansa?"

Nothing.

He found the bedside table by memory.

His phone was there where he had left it.

It was warm.

He stood in the dark with it in his hand, feeling the warmth of it the way you feel the side of a cup, and not understanding for a moment what warmth meant. Somebody had been holding it. Somebody had set it down recently enough that it had not had time to cool.

He thumbed the screen alive and swept its weak light across the room.

Empty bed. Empty corner. The plastic chair, empty.

He found the wall switch.

Light flooded the room.

Still empty.

Her perfume was everywhere. That was the thing he could not make sense of — the perfume, recent and close and thick in the air, as if she had only just been standing exactly where he was standing, as if she had stepped sideways into the wall. He could smell her and not find her. And there was only one door.

He dialled.

It rang once.

REJECTED.

He looked at the screen the way a man looks at a thing that has just done something impossible.

He tried again.

REJECTED.

The screen stayed lit in his hand. Under his own two calls, the log showed a number he did not recognise, called that night.

He looked at the number.

He did not look at it properly. He was frightened, and a frightened man does not read small print — he looks for the next thing to do.

He went back out into the yard.

"Mansa!"

Nothing. Not a window opened. Not a voice. He crossed to the Old Woman's door and knocked, and heard the chair creak inside, and then nothing at all.

"Auntie. Have you seen Mansa?"

A long pause. Then her voice, thin through the wood, irritable with sleep.

"I have heard nothing tonight. Not even the gate."

He stood in the dark yard with that.

Not even the gate.

He put the phone in his pocket and went out to the taxi.

The engine caught on the second try, coughing, then settling.

He drove slowly at first, along the main road with the windows down, saying her name at the empty roadside the way a man says a name into a room he already knows is empty. Then faster, out towards the unlit stretches where he thought she might have walked.

He would drive every street in Mallam Town if he had to.

He did not know that by going to look for his wife, he was driving into the thing that would take everything else.

# Chapter 3 — Dangerous Lane

Dangerous Lane was not a lane, exactly. It was the stretch of road between the last compound and the cemetery wall, where the streetlights gave up and the bush came close on both sides.

Everyone in Mallam Town knew not to stop there after dark.

Kwame drove it anyway, leaning forward over the wheel, scanning both sides for a flash of her cloth, her shape walking, anything. He had driven it a hundred times at that hour and had never once thought about it — which is what twenty years does to a man. It makes the dangerous ordinary.

He was thinking her name so hard that he almost did not see the headlights.

They came up behind him too close and too fast.

He checked the mirror. Moved over. They came with him.

Before he could decide what that meant, a second vehicle cut in ahead of him and swung across the lane, and there was nowhere left to go.

Kwame stopped.

His hands stayed on the wheel.

Two men and a woman got out of the car in front. Unhurried. That was the thing he would remember afterwards — how unhurried they were, the way people are when they have done this before and know exactly how long it takes.

One of the men carried a tyre iron. The woman held a pistol low against her thigh, almost casual, like a handbag.

Kwame rolled down his window. Years of difficult passengers had taught him a certain calm.

"I don't have any —"

"Step out," the Woman said.

He did.

There was no argument left in him, and it was not because he had given up. It was because he had done this calculation a hundred times — with angry passengers, with drunk men at the rank, with his own wife shouting at him at twenty to midnight — and the mathematics had never once favoured pride.

Somebody took his phone out of his pocket without asking.

"Face the car."

He faced the car.

What followed was fast, and almost businesslike. Professional. A blow to the ribs — controlled, not wild. Another. Somebody grabbed the back of his shirt and it tore. A third man pinned his arms behind him before either hand was touched.

"Not the face," the Man with the Tyre Iron said.

"Leave his hands," the Woman said.

He did not understand why, then. He would understand later.

His shoulder went. He heard it go — a sound like a door being shut in another room — and then the ground came up and hit him, and something sharp took the skin above his left eye, and the rest of it happened somewhere below the level of thought.

Afterwards there was the sound of his own engine starting.

His own car. The car Mansa had bought him to make them money, pulling away into the dark with somebody else at the wheel.

He did not get up. He could not. For a long time he lay in the dust beside the road, listening to his own breathing, and to the insects, and to Mallam Town continuing somewhere beyond the lane, uncaring.

Then silence.

Not quiet. Mallam Town was never quiet. But the particular silence of a place where something has finished happening and the dust has not settled yet.

A few feet from his hand, face-down in the dust where somebody had dropped it in the hurry of leaving, a phone began to ring.

It rang twice, and stopped.

Nobody was there to hear it.

# Chapter 4 — Nene



She was out before the sun, because the bark she wanted came off the tree behind the cemetery, and it came off easier before the day got hot and the sap rose.

Nene was not her name, not really. Nene was what you called an old woman you were about to ask a favour of.

Her knees had their own opinion about the cemetery path. She told them what she thought of it and went on anyway. The basket was light going out, which was the only thing going out had to recommend it.

The road was the colour of nothing yet. Light enough to see the potholes. Not light enough to see into them.

There was a shape in the gutter on Dangerous Lane.

She had walked past shapes before. Mallam Town put things in its gutters and expected you to step over them. But this one lay at an angle no drunk man lies at, with one arm underneath him where an arm had no business being.

She set the basket down. It took her a while to kneel, and it hurt, and she said so to nobody.

A few feet away, face-down in the dust where somebody had dropped it in the hurry of leaving, lay a phone.

Nene looked at it for a moment. Then she picked it up and put it in the pocket of her cloth. A phone is a useful thing to have. And a man whose phone is gone is a man nobody can call, which suits somebody.

She turned him over by the good arm.

"Ei," she said. "Who did this to you?"

He did not answer. One eye was shut, his breathing was shallow, and there was dust in his mouth.

Then the light moved, just enough, and she saw his face.

This was Kwame. The taxi driver. The Peacemaker. The one they all said never raised his voice — who sat at the rank with a rag and a mirror and settled other people's quarrels the way other men shell groundnuts.

"So," Nene said to the empty road. "Even you."

She could not lift him. She was seventy-one and he was a grown man, and the arithmetic was not complicated.

What she could do was shout.

Two boys going to fetch water heard her and came running. Then a woman from the compound. Then — because this is how things happen in Mallam Town, quickly and without anybody arranging it — a man with a cart. They got him to her compound between them, the way you move a sack of cement, except more carefully.

Her room smelled of bark. Herbs hung in bundles from a line across the ceiling. There was a mortar in the corner, worn smooth on one side, and a shelf of small bottles, each with a name scratched into the glass.

She sent everyone out except the boy who looked least likely to faint.

The shoulder came first. A shoulder out of its socket will not wait, and it will not wait for anybody. She had set her nephew's the year he fell out of the mango tree, and two of the market women's, and one belonging to a man who never thanked her and never would.

"Hold him," she said.

She put her hands where they belonged and told him it would hurt, though he could not hear her, and pulled.

It went back with a sound that made her own bones ache in sympathy.

The boy made a noise and sat down hard on the floor.

"If you are going to be sick," Nene said, "be sick outside."

The ribs came next, and there was less she could do for ribs. Bruised, she thought, not broken — a broken rib has a sound to it when a man breathes, and his breathing was shallow but clean. She bound him with a length of cloth, not too tight.

The cut above the eye she cleaned with water she had boiled that morning and left to cool.

Afterwards she sat back on her heels and turned his hands over, palm up, the way you check a fish.

Not marked. Whoever had done this had left his hands alone. She did not know why, and then she did: a taxi driver needs his hands. Needs his face too, if he wants to work again.

These people were not animals. They were workers, doing a job.

Then she put two fingers against the side of his neck and held them there, counting to herself the way her mother had taught her to count a thing that was still running.

Slow. But there.

Then she held his head in both hands, carefully, and turned it, and looked into his eyes.

One pupil was sluggish. The other was not.

"If his head were bleeding inside," she said to the boy, "no leaf would help him. That one is for the hospital, and I will not pretend otherwise. Go and watch the road. If a car comes, tell me."

That is the whole of it, really. Knowing which things are yours.

Then the bark, pounded in the mortar worn smooth on one side, made into a paste, laid on the bruises and held with a clean cloth. It was not a cure. It was the difference between a body that swells and a body that does not, and anybody who tells you otherwise is selling something.

She checked his eyes every hour through the night. By dawn both pupils were the same size, and she let out a breath she had been holding since the gutter.

"You were lucky," she said to him, though he still could not hear her. "I was ready to send you."

---

Pain arrived before memory did.

A deep, sick throb in the ribs. A hot tightness around the left eye. And his right shoulder — his right shoulder was simply not there, and then it was, and then it hurt in a way he could not have described to anybody.

He tried to sit up. The room tilted. He fell back and said a word he had not said in twenty years.

"Lie still," somebody said. "You will open the cut."

The ceiling was not his ceiling. The light was wrong. Bundles of something hung from a line above him, and the room smelled of bark and of something else, green and crushed.

There was a small plastic chair. An old woman sat in it, asleep, or pretending.

He lay back and closed his eye that worked, and said, without meaning to:

"Mansa."

---

It was evening when he woke properly. Light the colour of dust coming through the window. The old woman was awake, grinding something in the corner, and did not turn around.

He lay still for a while and took stock.

He was alive. That seemed worth something, and he gave thanks for it quietly, the way he always had, to himself, without moving his lips.

The grinding stopped.

"The one who made the tree," she said, "and the one who made the leaf. You think they are different people?"

He did not know what to say to that.

She turned around then, and he saw her properly for the first time. Small. Seventy-odd. Hands that looked like they had spent a life doing things. A stick against the wall beside her that she plainly resented.

"I am Nene," she said. "You are in my compound. You were in the gutter on Dangerous Lane with no taxi and half your shirt gone, and I did not think it was a good night to leave you there."

"Why not the hospital?" Kwame said.

She shrugged.

"And then the police come," she said, "and they ask questions, and you are a man with bruises and no answers. I have watched that happen. I am not watching it again."

She went back to grinding.

Kwame lay still. His ribs ached when he breathed too deep. Outside, Mallam Town was going about its evening — a radio, a pot lid, somebody laughing too loud. Ordinary life, continuing, as if nothing had happened.

"I have to report it anyway," he said.

"I know," Nene said. "I will take you on Thursday. Today you cannot walk to that road and back."

He closed his eye that worked.

"Thursday," he said.

"Thursday," she said.

# Chapter 5 — The Report

Nene walked with him as far as the main road and stopped there.

"I am not going any further," she said. "I have been in that building once in my life, and once was plenty."

"Will you wait?"

"I will wait."

She sat down on the low wall by the drainage ditch, the way old women sit, as though the wall had been put there for her, and took a piece of bark from her pocket and began to work at it with her thumbnail.

Kwame went on alone.

The Mallam Town Police Post was a squat concrete building with paint peeling at the corners. A ceiling fan went round and round above the counter too slowly to matter, pushing hot air from one side of the room to the other.

Kwame leaned on the counter and moved stiffly, because every breath caught somewhere on the left side of his ribs.

The Desk Officer did not look up more than was necessary. He had a thick ledger open in front of him and a blue Biro that skipped.

"Make and year?"

"Toyota. Old one," Kwame said. "Dent on the rear passenger door. You'll know it before you ask the plate number."

The officer wrote. Kwame watched the Biro skip and thought: write it properly. Write it properly. This is the only thing anybody is ever going to write down.

"Colour?"

"Yellow. Faded."

"Where taken?"

"Dangerous Lane. About half past twelve Monday night. Three of them. Two men and a woman. They had a pistol."

The officer's head came up at that, and went down again.

"And the wife?"

Kwame opened his mouth.

How do you say it? How do you say to a man writing in a ledger: I went out to cool my head after an argument. I came back ten minutes later. She was gone and her perfume was still in the air. I called her twice and she rejected the call both times. Then I went out to look for her and they took the car and left me in the gutter, and now I do not know where either of them is.

You cannot fit that into the lines of a police form.

"She's missing," Kwame said finally. "Since Monday night."

The officer nodded and wrote. He said it the way men say things they do not expect to revisit.

"We'll log it."

Behind the counter, through a half-open office door, Kwame caught a glimpse of the senior officer.

Chief Inspector Nanfuri was in his forties and tired in the unremarkable way of a man with too many open files. He stood at his desk with the telephone at his ear and his back half turned, and he said into it, in a voice that was not unkind:

"I know. I know I said I would. Just — give me till Friday."

Then he glanced up. His eye went past Kwame to the counter and the ledger, and found nothing there that needed him, and he turned back to the wall.

Kwame had the strangest feeling, standing there — that the man in that room was carrying something. He could not have said what. It was in the set of the shoulders.

Then the moment passed, and the feeling went with it, and Kwame forgot about it entirely until much later, when there was no forgetting left to do.

The officer tore a slip off the pad and handed it over.

A case number.

It felt thin in his hand. Too thin to hold everything that had happened.

Nene was still on the wall when he came out. She did not ask how it had gone. She looked at his face, put the bark back in her pocket, and stood up, and they walked back to her compound together in the heat.

Five days passed.

Five days in which Kwame went to the rank and asked, and the drivers shook their heads, and Papa Kofi said the thing everybody says — "Chaley, maybe she went to her sister's, women do that" — and Kwame said she would have answered her phone, and Papa Kofi had no answer for that.

Five days in which Nene asked after Mansa the way old women do, which is to say between other words, while measuring bark or grinding something in the corner, so that nobody listening would have known she was asking anything at all.

Five days in which nobody from the Mallam Town Police Post came to the compound. Nobody walked the lane. Nobody knocked on a door.

On the fifth evening Kwame went back to the room.

He stood in the doorway for a long time with the light on.

The perfume was fading. It had been so strong that first night you could almost taste it, and now it was only there if you went looking for it, and soon it would not be there at all.

Nene was right, he thought.

What he did not think, because it did not occur to him, was that Nene was usually right.

# Chapter 6 — A Face on the Radio

The radio was always on.

It sat on the shelf in Nene's room between a tin of something and a bottle with a name scratched into the glass, and it played from the moment she got up until the batteries gave out, which they did most afternoons. Nene said she did not listen to it. She had it on the way other people have a door open.

That morning Kwame was trying to help.

He had got as far as lifting the pestle over the mortar with his right hand and bringing it down once, badly, before the shoulder said no.

"Sit," Nene said, without turning round.

"I have been sitting for a week."

"Then sit for two. The body is not a thing you hurry. I have watched men make themselves worse being in a hurry to be better."

He sat.

It was just after seven. The station played highlife for a while, and then the newsreader's voice came on, and Nene reached over and turned it up a little, the way people do when they are not really listening.

"—the body of a young woman was discovered early this morning in a vehicle abandoned near the Mallam Town cemetery road. Police say the deceased has been identified as the daughter of a senior officer attached to the Mallam Town Police Post. She was twenty-four years old. The vehicle is believed to have been used in an armed robbery on Monday night —"

The radio hissed. Nene slapped it with the flat of her hand, the way everybody in Ghana slaps a radio, and it came back.

"—appealing to the public for information —"

Kwame said: "A taxi."

Nene looked at him.

"What vehicle?" Kwame said. "Did they say what vehicle?"

The newsreader had moved on to fuel prices.

It came at eight, on the next bulletin, and by then the whole town was listening.

"The Ghana Police Service is seeking Kwame Asante, a taxi driver of Mallam Town, in connection with the robbery and the death of Efua Nanfuri, twenty-four, daughter of Chief Inspector Nanfuri of the Mallam Town Police Post."

His name. His age. The description of his taxi. And then the words *armed and dangerous*, and then a reward, and then the thing that made Kwame set the pestle down on the table very carefully, as though it were made of something that would break:

"Asante is known locally as 'The Peacemaker.' Members of the public are warned not to approach him. Anybody found to be sheltering him will be charged."

Kwame sat very still.

"Efua Nanfuri," he said. "The daughter of the man in that office."

"Yes," Nene said.

"He was standing there with the telephone at his ear while I was reporting my taxi stolen. It was his daughter. She was not even dead yet."

Nobody said anything for a while. The radio played an advertisement for a funeral home.

Then Kwame said:

"Monday night."

"What?"

"The robbery. Monday night." His mouth was dry. "I reported that taxi stolen on Thursday."

"Which Monday night?" Nene said.

Kwame opened his mouth. Then he counted, and the counting took him a while, and he did it on his fingers the way a man does when the numbers are the only thing holding him up.

"Not the Monday they took my car," he said. "The Monday after. I stood in that station on the Thursday and put my own name in their book. She died the Monday night four days after that."

Nene did not help him.

"I reported it *before*," Kwame said. "Nene — I reported that taxi stolen four days before they used it to kill her."

Nene looked at him. She did not say anything. But she stopped what she was doing with her hands, which for Nene was the same as shouting.

"I can't walk into a police station now," Kwame said.

"No," Nene said.

Not pity. Not shock. She said it the way she had said *sit* — the way a person says a thing that is simply true and therefore not worth arguing about.

He waited for whatever it is people say next. He could not have told you what that was either.

"Then we find out who did it ourselves," Nene said. "Slowly. Properly."

Kwame looked at her. She had gone back to the mortar, working with her left hand because the right was busy, or because she always did.

"How?" he said.

Nene did not answer for a moment. Then:

"People come here," she said. "They have been coming here for forty years. And people talk to me, because they think I am not really listening, and because what is said in this room does not leave it. I have been hearing things about those uncompleted houses behind the cemetery for a long time now."

Kwame stared at her.

"What things?"

Nene put the pestle down.

"Sit down, Kwame," she said. "This will take a while, and I am not young."

# Chapter 7 — What Nene Knows

He asked her the next morning how she knew things.

They were sitting outside in the shade of the low wall, Nene sorting leaves into three piles and Kwame doing what he had been told to do, which was nothing.

"People come here," she said, without looking up. "That is the whole of it. That is the whole method."

She sorted for a while.

"They come for a child with a fever. For a back. For a thing they will not go to the hospital about, because the hospital asks questions, and the hospital writes things down. They sit. They wait. And while they are waiting they talk — not to me, not really. To the room. To somebody who will not repeat it."

She picked up a leaf, looked at it, put it in the second pile.

"Nobody watches an old woman, Kwame. That is the whole use of me. People say things in front of a wall. People say things in front of a mirror. I have been a wall for forty years."

Kwame thought about that.

"So what have you heard?"

Nene got up — slowly, and he had learned by now not to help — and went inside. She came back with two small bottles and set them on the wall beside him.

Green glass. Each with a name scratched into it with the point of a nail.

"Kwesi," she said, touching the first. "Seventeen. Apprentice at the mechanic shop by the rank — the one in the blue container. You have bought a fan belt there."

Kwame nodded. He had bought more than a fan belt there.

"Ekow," she said, touching the second. "Nineteen. Same shop. Different mother. Not brothers. But they were the kind of friends who are."

She sat down again.

"A year ago last March, they stopped coming home."

"What happened to them?"

"Nobody knows. That is the thing. Nobody knows, because nobody looked. Their mothers went to the police. The police wrote it down — I have seen a ledger before, Kwame. I know what writing looks like when nothing is going to come of it. Then their mothers came to me."

Kwame looked at the two bottles.

"What did they want?"

"Sleeplessness." Nene said the word flatly. "That is what they asked for. But a woman does not come to me for sleeplessness because she cannot sleep. She comes because she lies in the dark thinking about a boy who has not come home, and after a while the thinking itself is the sickness."

She turned the first bottle over in her hands.

"What did you give them?"

"What was there to give? Something to make them sleep. I am not the police, Kwame. I am a woman with a shelf."

Six months ago, Nene said, a man had vanished.

Forty-something. A construction worker, one of the men putting up the uncompleted houses behind the cemetery — the same ones Kwame's taxi had been found beside. One Friday he did not come home. His wife came to Nene. Then his mother.

"I made the same medicine," she said. "And I was angry while I was making it, and it did not help anybody."

Kwame sat with it for a while.

"And now they are looking," he said slowly. "Because of the commander's daughter."

"Yes."

"Because she was somebody."

"Yes."

Nene looked at him then, and her face did something Kwame had not seen it do.

# Chapter 14 — Dawn at the Uncompleted House

They went out at first light.

Kwame had expected a dozen people. There were a hundred before they reached the main road, and more coming out of the compounds as they passed, and by the cemetery wall it was not a crowd any more, it was a town.

Nene led it, leaning on her stick, going slower than anybody.

# Chapter 15 — The Okada

It happened while the crowd was still there.

Kwame heard it before he saw it — the whine of a small engine coming up the track too fast, the particular sound an okada makes when the rider is not thinking about the road.

He saw the cement and the wire and the generator under its sheet. He turned and ran.

---

The crowd did not close.

That was the thing Kwame remembered afterwards, and the thing he went on remembering for years — that not one person in a hundred put out a hand. No shout. No stone. Nothing at all.

They simply parted, the way a crowd parts, and let him through. Because there was nowhere left for him to go, and everybody standing there knew it.

He got back on the bike.

It fishtailed at once on the loose gravel of the track — the track he had never had surfaced in four years, because a surfaced track is a track people remember — and went down hard on its side, and Sarpong went down with it, and the bike went on without him in a long skid of sparks and dust.

The car coming up behind him was not going fast.

That did not matter.

The sound was not a scream. It was a noise Kwame had heard once, years ago, at the rank, and had never managed to forget: the ordinary, muffled sound of a body in the way of something heavy.

Then dust, and quiet, and a hundred people standing absolutely still.

---

Nene was the first to move.

She went forward leaning on her stick, and she knelt — which took her a while, and hurt, and she said so to nobody — and she put two fingers against the side of Sarpong's neck.

Exactly as she had done for Kwame in the gutter.

Somebody behind her said it, breathless and half-pleased:

"It is finished for him."

Nene did not look up.

"It is not finished for him," she said. "It is beginning."

She shifted her weight, and Kwame saw what it cost her to stay down there, and she did not get up.

"He is still a man. Somebody call the hospital."

They said afterwards that he would not walk again.

Kwame never heard anybody say they were sorry about it. He never heard anybody say they were glad.

Inspector Adzo Mensah took Sarpong's statement where he lay on the track, with her notebook on her knee and the crowd standing well back, because that was her job and she did it properly. "My knees," she said, to nobody in particular. "I have been telling you people about my knees for a year and nobody listens to me."

A child ran past and she told him to slow down, and he slowed down.

That is the whole point of her, he thought.

Two men at the far end looked up. Papa Kofi said something to Mr. Tee. Somewhere a dog started up.

This time, everybody heard.
